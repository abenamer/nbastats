# Player-level, simulation and machine-learning NBA prediction models: accuracy gain over Elo and the market

Method note: the papers and pages could not be opened in the research session, so all findings below come from search-engine result snippets and summaries of the cited pages, not from reading the full papers. Numbers are quoted as they appeared in those snippets. Where a snippet only said "beats X" without a figure, that is recorded as a gap rather than estimated. FiveThirtyEight stopped publishing in 2025, so its pages are archival.

## Key question 1: Which published or well-documented models use player availability, lineups or possession simulation to predict NBA games, and how did they do versus Elo-type baselines and Vegas?

### Takeaway
Player-based models (RAPTOR/CARMELO blends, penalized-regression RAPM-style ratings, player-level convolutional nets) and possession/play simulators exist and are documented, but almost none publish a clean head-to-head accuracy table against an Elo baseline and against the closing line on the same games. Where comparisons exist, the richer models land at roughly 68-74% game-outcome accuracy, in the same band as Elo and the closing line (about 67-71% favorite accuracy), and the one quantified comparison of a player-level CNN against Pinnacle closing odds had the model *less* accurate than the bookmaker (0.68 vs 0.70).

### Cited Findings
- FiveThirtyEight's NBA model started as pure Elo; Elo uses only results, margin of victory and game location, so it "wouldn't be able to pick up on injuries, trades, or rest." RAPTOR (Robust Algorithm using Player Tracking and On/Off Ratings) was introduced for 2019-20 as a player-based metric, and from 2020 the forecast was "based entirely on our NBA player projections" with a separate pure-Elo forecast kept for comparison — [FiveThirtyEight methodology](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-our-nba-predictions-work/); [FlowingData on the RAPTOR launch](https://flowingdata.com/2019/10/14/fivethirtyeight-launches-new-nba-metric-for-predications/)
- FiveThirtyEight said in-house testing found blending Elo with its talent (RAPTOR) rating "improves the forecasts," with the Elo weight depending on how much of the rating was built by players still on the roster; the team's full-strength rating and its injury/rest-adjusted current rating were combined with Elo. No metric or magnitude was given in the pages the search returned — [FiveThirtyEight 2020-21 season preview](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/what-to-watch-for-in-the-2020-21-nba-season)
- A mirror of FiveThirtyEight text states that "extensive testing during the 2020 offseason showed that giving Elo about 35 percent weight (and RAPTOR talent 65 percent) produces the best predictive results for future games, on average," and that the program "can spit out separate talent ratings for every single game on a team's schedule" from the injury/transaction list. This is from a scraped copy on an unrelated domain and should be verified against the original — [text mirror of the FiveThirtyEight page, unverified](https://www.bit-a.jp/1bf108/fivethirtyeight-nba-prediction-accuracy)
- Omidiran (2013), "Penalized Regression Models for the NBA" (arXiv 1301.3523), fits a Subspace Prior Regression on player ratings for 2010-11 and reports that, "with a sufficiently large training set, the model outperforms" the Las Vegas lines; the abstract gives no win rate or error figure — [arXiv 1301.3523](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3523); [HTML version](https://arxiv.org/html/1301.3523v1)
- Petridis and Pelechrinis (2026), Lineup Regularized Adjusted Plus-Minus (L-RAPM, arXiv 2601.15000): an average lineup has only "approximately 25-30 possessions" of data; L-RAPM "provides improved predictive power than the currently used baseline, with the gain growing as lineup samples shrink." No betting-line benchmark appears in the snippets — [arXiv 2601.15000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15000v1)
- Hubáček, Šourek and Železný (2019), International Journal of Forecasting 35(2):783-796, use a convolutional network over player-level statistics plus portfolio-theory bet sizing and deliberately reduce correlation with bookmaker odds; it "yielded positive cumulative profits in experiments with NBA data from seasons 2007-2014 systematically, as opposed to alternative methods tested." Accuracy of model vs bookmaker is not in the abstract — [IDA CTU paper page](https://ida.fel.cvut.cz/papers/hubacek2019exploiting.html); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v35y2019i2p783-796.html)
- An experimental review of betting strategies (arXiv 2107.08827) re-ran that player-level CNN on an NBA dataset of about 16,000 games (2000-2015) with Pinnacle closing-line money-line odds: model accuracy 0.68 versus bookmaker accuracy 0.70; the authors note "the model is not as accurate as the bookmaker, and is thus in a general KL-disadvantage" — [arXiv 2107.08827](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08827)
- APBR forum posts (anecdotal, not peer-reviewed): one user claims a player-based model reached "a log loss matching Vegas (~0.59)" using players with 5+ minutes; another argues RAPM-type approaches are limited to explaining about 70% of variance even with three years of data — [APBR thread](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?p=43120); [APBR RAPM playoff thread](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?p=19845)
- Possession/play simulators: Manisera, Zuccolotto and Ley (2025) simulate NBA and EuroLeague games 2000-2020 at the level of individual plays (penalized Poisson/negative binomial play counts, compound Poisson score) and say accuracy is "highly competitive to, and often better than, the literature" with no percentage in the snippet — [Unibs record](https://iris.unibs.it/handle/11379/630105). A Duke thesis models each team's possession time and points per possession as an alternating renewal-reward process, simulates all 1,209 games of 2012-13 and benchmarks against record, Pythagorean win% and bookmaker odds (numbers not in snippet) — [Duke thesis](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/items/728b4f63-0163-44e6-b2ab-6eb3f5650010). A 2012 International Journal of Forecasting paper builds a homogeneous Markov possession model from play-by-play and benchmarks it against logit regression, a latent-strength rating and bookmaker odds (numbers not in snippet) — [IAOR record](https://iaorifors.com/paper/76013). A 2023 Johns Hopkins student possession-based scoring model reported about 60% accuracy for 2022-23 versus about 65% for existing models — [JHU project PDF](https://designday.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/formidable/18/Predictive-Model-and-Algorithm-for-NBA-Games-2023-1-1.pdf)
- A 2012 Inpredictable post notes that "stat-based models do not handle injuries and other recent developments very well" and backs out a player's market value from the gap between the market line and a stat projection (Deron Williams about 2.5 points) — [Inpredictable](https://inpredictable.com/2012/03/new-feature-todays-games.html)
- Neil Paine's post-FiveThirtyEight model defaults to a "Composite Mode" that blends ratings-based simulations with FanDuel implied probabilities, explicitly because "a statistical power rating-based system cannot readily account for" injuries and trades; his 2025-26 model keeps separate regular-season and playoff Elo ratings — [Paine 2024-25 forecast](https://neilpaine.substack.com/p/2024-25-nba-forecast); [Paine 2025-26 Elo forecast](https://neilpaine.substack.com/p/2025-26-nba-elo-forecast-and-player)
- Migliorati (2021, arXiv 2111.09695), deep-learning models with cross-validation over 16 NBA regular seasons: single-feature models using Elo or relative victory frequency "achieve a better quality of fit than models built on box-score predictors such as the Four Factors" — [arXiv 2111.09695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09695v1)
- Baseline for the market: over five NBA seasons "only 67.01% of the teams Vegas has picked to be the favorites have actually won" (student project) — [Yale CSEC project](https://csec.yale.edu/node/110); Vegas-based accuracy about 0.71 for 2016 regular and post-season games in the "Wages of wins" paper, whose best model reached about 0.74 (table garbled in snippet) — [arXiv 1702.05982](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.05982)

### Inferences
- The strongest documented operator of a player-availability model (FiveThirtyEight) chose a 35/65 Elo/RAPTOR blend rather than replacing Elo, and even the player-based model kept Elo weight; this is consistent with the gain from player data being real but partial.
- Across the sources, player-level, lineup and possession models all cluster in the 68-74% accuracy band that Elo and the closing line also occupy. No source in this search showed a player-based model with a sustained accuracy edge over the closing line; the single quantified comparison went the other way (0.68 vs 0.70).
- Lineup-level models face a hard data problem (25-30 possessions per lineup) that limits their predictive advantage over team- or player-level aggregates, which is why their reported gains are over an RAPM baseline rather than over Vegas.

### Gaps
- No source gave FiveThirtyEight's own RAPTOR-vs-Elo accuracy, Brier or log-loss comparison; the "improves the forecasts" claim is unquantified in the pages returned.
- Omidiran's exact accuracy/ATS record against Vegas and the number of training games needed were not in the abstract.
- Hubáček et al.'s accuracy and ROI figures (2007-2014) were not retrievable; only the independent 0.68 vs 0.70 re-run is quantified.
- The Markov (2012 IJF), renewal-reward (Duke 2013) and play-level (2025) simulators all benchmark against bookmaker odds, but none of the snippets contained the resulting accuracy tables.
- Nothing was found for ESPN BPI game-level accuracy versus Vegas, nor any Dunks & Threes / DRIP backtest against the spread.

## Key question 2: What do machine-learning studies report as best achievable accuracy, how often do they fail to beat the closing line, and what methodological flaws recur?

### Takeaway
Reported ML accuracies for NBA game outcomes cluster at 65-74%, with a frequently cited 74-75% "glass ceiling" (Zimmermann and co-authors; Loeffelholz et al. 74.33%); rigorous studies that compare against the closing line find the model at or below the bookmaker (0.68 vs 0.70), while higher claims (72-87%) typically lack a market benchmark or use in-game or end-of-game information.

### Cited Findings
- Loeffelholz, Bednar and Bauer (2009): feed-forward neural network on four shooting variables picked winners 74.33% of the time versus USA Today experts at 68.67%, on roughly 620-650 games of 2007-08 (secondary sources disagree on exact sample size) — [arXiv 1912.11762 review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11762); [arXiv 2111.09695](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.09695v1)
- Zimmermann and co-authors report "there seems to be an upper limit to predictive accuracy for match outcomes, at around 74%-75%" for college basketball and list the NBA (74.33%) as falling in a similar region; a companion paper "Chance and the predictive limit in basketball" derives season-specific accuracy limits by clustering team profiles with schedule information and states similar results hold for the NBA (exact NBA limits not in snippets) — [arXiv 1310.3607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.3607); [Zimmermann chance-in-bball PDF](https://zimmermanna.users.greyc.fr/papers/conferences/chance-in-bball.pdf); [arXiv 1508.04688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.04688)
- Aoki, Assunção and Vaz de Melo, "Luck is Hard to Beat" (KDD 2017), 198 leagues/1,503 seasons: the NBA underdog wins with probability about 0.36, home advantage adds about 0.09; "luck is substantially present even in the most competitive championships, which partially explains why sophisticated and complex feature-based models hardly beat simple models" — [arXiv 1706.02447](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02447)
- Cheng et al. (2016, Entropy): maximum-entropy NBAME model "correctly predict[s] the winning team 74.4 percent of the time" for NBA playoffs — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/623c0aaeaa7a479c8ed26b9a7f39630d)
- "Wages of wins: could an amateur make money from match outcome predictions?" (arXiv 1702.05982): best model about 0.74 vs Vegas-based about 0.71 on 2016 NBA regular and post-season games (table garbled; which figure is the Vegas baseline is uncertain) — [arXiv 1702.05982](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.05982)
- Review of NBA prediction work: box-score methods roughly 67% (Naive Bayes) and 74% (neural networks); 66-72% for regular seasons across approaches — [Unibs Platjouw PDF](https://bodai.unibs.it/bdsports/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2020/11/Platjouw-Emiel.pdf)
- Bryant University honors thesis: Gaussian Naive Bayes averaged 65.1% — [Houde thesis](https://digitalcommons.bryant.edu/honors_data_science/1/)
- Long-sequence LSTM paper (arXiv 2512.08591) claims 72.35% accuracy but cites a roughly 55% figure for betting lines, which conflicts with the 67-71% market baselines above and should be treated skeptically — [arXiv 2512.08591](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.08591)
- Player-level CNN (Hubáček et al.) re-run on 2000-2015 data with Pinnacle closing odds: 0.68 model vs 0.70 bookmaker — [arXiv 2107.08827](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08827)
- MLSA21 workshop slides: game-outcome prediction with mean accuracy 69.88%; season-level picks "matched betting odds for the champion and outperform[ed] ELO for all playoff rounds" (slide text only, methodology not visible) — [MLSA21 slides](https://www.ida.liu.se/~patla00/talks/MLSA21.pdf)
- Market efficiency literature: point-spread and totals markets for 2000-01 to 2007-08 were found efficient — [The Sport Journal](https://thesportjournal.org/article/nba-gambling-inefficiencies-a-second-look/); a Miami University thesis using 2013-14 to 2016-17 data reports profitable strategies "amplified given the presence of lower markups" at online books, so the evidence is mixed — [Lust thesis](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=miami1533158563565605&disposition=inline)
- A systematic review of ML in sports betting (arXiv 2410.21484) frames the closing line as the standard reference and notes little evidence of bettors consistently beating it — [arXiv 2410.21484](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21484v1)
- Methodology: feature leakage "arises when features contain information that is not available at the time of prediction"; a 2025 BRACIS study found leakage inflated performance in nearly half the datasets tested, with feature selection the strongest distortion; Kapoor and Narayanan (2022) found 329 papers across 17 fields whose findings did not hold after leakage correction — [BRACIS paper](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/bracis/article/view/40816); [DataEval leakage concepts](https://dataeval.readthedocs.io/en/latest/concepts/Leakage.html)
- Migliorati stresses features were "ex ante calculated" and evaluated with cross-validation over 16 seasons, and finds single-feature Elo models fit better than Four Factors box-score models — [arXiv 2111.09695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09695v1)
- Unsourced claims of 87% accuracy with ensemble models appear in a podcast summary with no methodology — [Audible podcast listing](https://www.audible.co.uk/podcast/EP-47-AI-Revolutionizes-Basketball-NBA-Analytics-2020-2025/B0GYXZ6FGB)

### Inferences
- The recurring 74-75% figure is best read as a practical ceiling for pre-game outcome accuracy driven by chance, not as a target that better features reliably reach; the closing line itself sits at roughly 67-71% depending on season and sample, so "beating Vegas" is a 1-3 percentage-point game.
- Studies claiming well above 75% almost always either use in-game or same-game box-score features (leakage), report in-sample or cross-validated fits over seasons rather than forward-chained holdouts, or compare against a mis-stated market baseline (for example the 55% claim).
- The one rigorous player-level model with a closing-line comparison lost on accuracy and only profited by decorrelating from the bookmaker and sizing bets, which is a statement about market exploitation, not forecasting superiority.

### Gaps
- No systematic count of "how often ML papers fail to beat the closing line" was found; the evidence is a handful of individual studies.
- Exact numbers for the Zimmermann NBA predictive limit, the Migliorati Elo vs Four Factors accuracies and the Hubáček accuracy table were not retrievable.
- No NBA-specific published critique of leakage in box-score-based prediction papers was found; the leakage points above are general ML literature.

## Key question 3: What is known about the value of injury and rest information for prediction accuracy?

### Takeaway
Market-implied values put top stars at roughly 3.5-5.5 points of spread and typical rotation players at about 1-2.5 points, and peer-reviewed work on 10,555 games shows opening lines are biased when a quality player is absent but closing lines fully price the absence; no study found quantifies the accuracy gain from adding injury features to a team-rating model.

### Cited Findings
- "Player absence and betting lines in the NBA" (Finance Research Letters, 2015): nine seasons, 10,555 games, 5,628 with at least one player absent; "substantial biases when a (high-quality) player is absent" in opening lines, "all biases are removed by game time," and evidence that "the market slightly overreacts to player absence, but the returns to this strategy are not great enough to overcome the commission" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612315000227)
- Oddsmaker point values for missing stars (2025 playoffs): Nikola Jokic 5.5 points, Shai Gilgeous-Alexander and Victor Wembanyama 4, Luka Doncic 3.5; Spurs moved from 1.5-point favorites without Wembanyama to 5.5-point favorites with him in one game — [theScore](https://www.thescore.com/nba/news/3531319/nba-playoff-injury-woes-how-much-are-stars-worth-to-their-teams); Joel Embiid about 3.5 points and Tyler Herro about 1 point in an earlier playoff — [theScore round 2 piece](https://www.thescore.com/nba/news/2640209)
- Market-implied value of Deron Williams about 2.5 points, derived from the gap between the Vegas line and a stat-based projection (2012) — [Inpredictable](https://inpredictable.com/2012/03/new-feature-todays-games.html)
- Action Network: stars are "worth their full value to the spread in key games that are expected to be close," less in expected blowouts; a quoted bettor says the public "always overreacts to superstar absences"; a questionable-to-out change can swing a line "dramatically in 90 minutes" — [Action Network player values 2018-19](https://www.actionnetwork.com/nba/nba-player-point-spread-values-2018-19-james-durant-curry-harden); [Action Network injuries guide](https://www.actionnetwork.com/education/how-injuries-affect-betting-lines-a-guide-to-market-movement)
- FiveThirtyEight's RAPTOR-era forecast computed separate talent ratings for every game from the injury list by reallocating minutes (mirror page, unverified) — [text mirror of the FiveThirtyEight page, unverified](https://www.bit-a.jp/1bf108/fivethirtyeight-nba-prediction-accuracy); its CARM-Elo model added travel penalties (about 16 CARM-Elo points for a Boston-to-Los Angeles leg) and an altitude bonus (about 47 points for Denver); the basic Elo home court is a constant 100 Elo points, about 3.5 NBA points, and a dynamic version "made almost no difference" — [How we calculate NBA Elo ratings](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-we-calculate-nba-elo-ratings); [How our 2015-16 NBA predictions work](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-our-2015-16-nba-predictions-work)
- FiveThirtyEight on rest (2017 Cavs-Raptors): "the simple fact that the Cavs are the better team" mattered more than rest — [FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/rest-wont-help-the-cavs-and-they-wont-need-any-help-to-beat-the-raptors)
- EvanMiya (college model, included as the only backtest statement found): historical predictions "had no injury adjustments, which likely made them perform slightly worse than they would with current injury data" — no magnitude given — [EvanMiya backtesting](https://blog.evanmiya.com/i/138347906/backtesting)
- Injury forecasting rather than game prediction: the METIC deep-learning model predicts NBA injuries from past injuries, activity and statistics and "performs significantly better than other traditional machine learning approaches" — [Journal of Sports Analytics record](https://content.iospress.com/articles/journal-of-sports-analytics/jsa200529); a student project found a 5-10 game sliding window best for availability prediction with LightGBM — [student project report](https://hajim.rochester.edu/ece/sites/zduan/teaching/ece408/projects/2025/MaxMcClung_NBAInjuryRiskAndAvailabilityAnalysis_FinalReport.pdf)
- NBA-commissioned load-management study: players did not show a lower injury rate when rested — [NBA news](https://api-hub.nba.com/news/nba-sends-data-load-management-study)
- Betting-blog claims that a star absence moves lines 3-6 points or 2-5 points are unsourced practitioner estimates — [topendsports](https://www.topendsports.com/betting-guides/sport-specific/nba/strategy.htm); [pointbetbasketball](https://pointbetbasketball.com/articles/nba-injury-impact-betting/)

### Inferences
- An MVP-level absence is worth about 1.0-1.5 team-Elo-equivalents of home court (3.5-5.5 points versus 3.5 points for 100 Elo), so on a game where a star sits a team-rating model is mis-set by more than its home-court term; the accuracy value of availability data therefore concentrates in the roughly half of games with an absence, and within those mostly on star absences (estimate, derived from the cited point values).
- Because closing lines already price absences fully, an availability-aware model closes most of its gap to the market but cannot exceed it on this information; the market's residual over-reaction is too small to clear the vig.
- Rest/schedule effects are small relative to talent gaps (FiveThirtyEight's qualitative conclusion, and the 16-point travel penalty is about one-sixth of home court).

### Gaps
- No study was found that reports accuracy, Brier or log loss with and without injury features for NBA game prediction, so the accuracy gain from availability data is unquantified in the literature retrieved.
- Nylon Calculus and Cleaning the Glass pieces on injury adjustments did not surface in the searches.
- The 35/65 blend and minutes-reallocation descriptions come from a mirror page, not the original FiveThirtyEight article.

## Key question 4: How did FiveThirtyEight's RAPTOR-based forecasts compare with Elo-only forecasts in their own and independent evaluations?

### Takeaway
FiveThirtyEight only ever stated qualitatively that blending RAPTOR talent with Elo "improves the forecasts" (settling at about 35% Elo / 65% RAPTOR per a mirrored text); independent evaluations compared FiveThirtyEight with betting markets rather than RAPTOR with Elo, and found no statistically significant difference on NBA playoff-berth Brier scores and only a slight market edge on game outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- FiveThirtyEight kept a pure-Elo forecast alongside the RAPTOR-based forecast and reported that blending improved forecasts, without metrics — [FiveThirtyEight 2020-21 preview](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/what-to-watch-for-in-the-2020-21-nba-season); [methodology](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-our-nba-predictions-work/)
- Mirrored FiveThirtyEight text: 2020 offseason testing found about 35% Elo / 65% RAPTOR "produces the best predictive results for future games, on average" (unverified mirror) — [text mirror of the FiveThirtyEight page, unverified](https://www.bit-a.jp/1bf108/fivethirtyeight-nba-prediction-accuracy)
- FiveThirtyEight's cross-domain self-evaluation: of 5,589 events given about a 70% chance, 71% occurred; of 55,853 events at about 5%, about 4% occurred; Brier skill scores are reported per forecast in an interactive (not NBA-specific in the snippets) — [When We Say 70 Percent](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/when-we-say-70-percent-it-really-means-70-percent)
- Bryan Shalloway (Dec 2021), FiveThirtyEight vs betting markets on NBA playoff berths, Brier scores from archived futures odds: FiveThirtyEight "seem[s] to hold-up OK against betting markets" and "quick t-tests showed no statistically significant difference"; the motivating gap was a 27% Lakers playoff probability versus a -460 market line — [Shalloway blog](https://www.bryanshalloway.com/2021/12/17/predicting-nba-playoff-berths-fivethirtyeight-vs-betting-markets/); [R-bloggers repost](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2021/12/predicting-nba-playoff-berths-fivethirtyeight-vs-betting-markets/)
- Albert Kuo (Jan 2022) evaluated FiveThirtyEight's NBA playoff models 2016-2020, separating calibration from accuracy (results not in snippets) — [Kuo blog](https://blog.albertkuo.me/post/2022-01-21-how-good-is-fivethirtyeight-s-nba-prediction-model/)
- A multi-sport comparison found betting markets "tended to slightly outperform FiveThirtyEight across all three sports" on a small game-outcome sample — [Mike's blog](https://www.mikesblog.net/p/march-madness-forecasts-fivethirtyeight)
- FiveThirtyEight and ESPN BPI 2022-23 season forecasts were compared side by side (agreement/disagreement, not accuracy) — [FiveThirtyEight vs ESPN](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/where-fivethirtyeight-and-espns-2022-23-nba-forecasts-agree-and-disagree)
- ESPN's 2008-2012 forecast panel of aggregated predictions "beat Vegas over a five-year span in basketball" on season win totals (ESPN's own claim) — [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/page/ESPN-Forecast-track-record-130415/nba-espn-forecast-proved-most-accurate-prediction-system)
- A 2026 arXiv paper scoring in-game models by sequential Kelly/Bayesian credibility found FiveThirtyEight's in-game model gained +13.8% net credibility over 84 games and 31,000+ plays of the 2023 playoffs against another in-game model (in-game, not pre-series) — [arXiv 2602.09982](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.09982)
- Nate Silver, 2023: "I'm without the RAPTOR projections that we usually published" after leaving FiveThirtyEight — [Silver Bulletin](https://www.natesilver.net/p/nba-eastern-conference-preview)

### Inferences
- The fact that an Elo-only forecast was retained as a public comparison, and that the best blend still gave Elo about a third of the weight, implies RAPTOR's marginal information over Elo was meaningful but not dominant; the magnitude was never disclosed.
- Independent evaluations place the RAPTOR-era FiveThirtyEight model statistically indistinguishable from the futures market on playoff berths and slightly behind the market on games, which bounds the achievable gain of a player-based model over Elo at "closes most of the gap to the market, does not pass it."

### Gaps
- No FiveThirtyEight page returned by the searches reports a Brier, log-loss or accuracy comparison of RAPTOR vs Elo forecasts, and the "How Good Are Our Forecasts" NBA-specific Brier skill score was not retrievable.
- The Shalloway and Kuo numeric results (Brier values per season) were not in the snippets.

## Key question 5: For playoff series and championship forecasts, what methods exist and what evidence is there on accuracy versus simple Elo-based series probabilities?

### Takeaway
Series methods range from independent-game binomials through home/away Bernoulli models, series-state Markov chains and logistic models with momentum terms to full Monte Carlo season simulation; the one rigorous NBA finding is that naive Monte Carlo playoff probabilities are systematically overconfident across 20 seasons unless game-to-game error correlation is modeled, and no source gives a direct accuracy comparison of richer series models against Elo-based series probabilities.

### Cited Findings
- "Misadventures in Monte Carlo" (Journal of Sports Analytics): Monte Carlo season models for 20 NBA seasons, each evaluated out-of-time, "collectively demonstrating systematic and substantial overconfidence in playoff probabilities, which can be eliminated by incorporating error correlation" — [JSA-170220](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/JSA-170220)
- NBA Finals logistic regression with home-court and momentum terms (JORS 2012): "negative serial correlation exists such that the team that wins one game is more likely to lose the next game of the series"; the per-game probabilities were used to compare playoff formats — [JORS 2012 abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1057/jors.2011.92); [IAOR record](https://iaorifors.com/paper/77709)
- Series-state Markov chains (2008, MLB data): transition probabilities depending on current series score "improve significantly on probability models based solely on home-away game dependence"; a two-parameter version ignoring venue and using only whether the home-advantage team is the historical favorite also worked well — [BGSU publication 1](https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/asor_pub/1)
- A home/away Bernoulli model "is preferable to the simpler, well-studied but ill-fitting binomial model that ignores home-field advantage" — [BGSU publication 2](https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/asor_pub/2)
- Standard best-of-7 computation treats games as i.i.d. with one per-game probability — [R-bloggers best-of-7](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2019/04/probability-of-winning-a-best-of-7-series/)
- Deshpande and Jensen (2016) Bayesian linear regression for in-game win probability assumes independence of observations (per-game, not within-series) — [arXiv 2301.04001 survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.04001)
- MLSA21 slides claim season-level picks "matched betting odds for the champion and outperform[ed] ELO for all playoff rounds" (methodology not visible) — [MLSA21 slides](https://www.ida.liu.se/~patla00/talks/MLSA21.pdf)
- FiveThirtyEight vs market on playoff berths: no statistically significant Brier difference (see KQ4) — [Shalloway](https://www.bryanshalloway.com/2021/12/17/predicting-nba-playoff-berths-fivethirtyeight-vs-betting-markets/)
- Neil Paine now maintains separate regular-season and playoff Elo ratings because regular-season Elo reflects "day-to-day form" with "scheduling, injuries and random swings" — [Paine 2025-26](https://neilpaine.substack.com/p/2025-26-nba-elo-forecast-and-player)
- Informal single-season track records: a 2025 ML-with-Elo-inputs model (10,000 simulations) went 8-for-8 in round one and missed two second-round series — [NBA Insights substack](https://nbainsights.substack.com/p/perfect-8-for-8-start-predicting); an Elo-only 20,000-simulation public model states it does "not take into account injuries or roster changes" — [Max Moacanin forecast](https://maxmoacanin.substack.com/p/2024-25-nba-forecast)
- Cheng et al. max-entropy model: 74.4% playoff game accuracy — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/623c0aaeaa7a479c8ed26b9a7f39630d)

### Inferences
- For a project running an Elo Monte Carlo, the documented failure mode is not low per-game accuracy but overconfidence of aggregated series/championship probabilities when rating error is treated as independent across games; adding correlated rating uncertainty is the evidence-backed fix.
- Within-series effects that the literature supports are home/away sequencing and series state; momentum appears as negative serial correlation in Finals data, which argues against "hot hand" adjustments.
- Richer championship forecasts (RAPTOR-based) were indistinguishable from the futures market on berths, so the ceiling for series/championship forecast quality is set by the market, and Elo-based forecasts' main deficit is roster/injury awareness rather than the series mechanics.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies the size of the Monte Carlo overconfidence (for example calibration slope or Brier difference) or compares a Bayesian within-series updating model against Elo series probabilities on NBA data.
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of series-level forecast accuracy (Brier or log loss per series) for any NBA model was found.
- FiveThirtyEight's playoff-specific Elo adjustments (playoff experience, rating multipliers) were not in the pages returned.
