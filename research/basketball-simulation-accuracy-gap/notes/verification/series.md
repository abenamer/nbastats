## 1. Demsyn-Jones 2019, "Misadventures in Monte Carlo"

### Bibliographic facts (PDF p.1)
- Author: Richard Demsyn-Jones, Analyst, Trust & Safety, Google (single author; corresponding e-mail rdj@google.com).
- Venue: Journal of Sports Analytics 5 (2019) 1–15, DOI 10.3233/JSA-170220, IOS Press.
- Copyright/licence line, p.1: "2215-020X/19/$35.00 © 2019 – IOS Press and the authors. All rights reserved" and "This article is published online with Open Access and distributed under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution Non-Commercial License (CC BY-NC 4.0)."
- Citation: Demsyn-Jones 2019 is correct.
- `research/papers/README.md` item 9 calls it "paywalled: link only; the authors are on the journal page". Wrong on both counts: it is CC BY-NC 4.0 open access (redistributable in this repository under the README's own rule) and the author is Richard Demsyn-Jones.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. Report line 60 and notes line 127: "a Journal of Sports Analytics study of Monte Carlo season models for 20 NBA seasons, each evaluated out of time, found 'systematic and substantial overconfidence in playoff probabilities, which can be eliminated by incorporating error correlation'".
   - Quote: confirmed verbatim, abstract p.1.
   - "20 NBA seasons": the abstract says 20, but the body says 22 throughout: "built separately ahead of each of 22 NBA seasons and evaluated on each out-of-time" (p.2); "22 models" (p.9); "22 years of attempted modeling, we end up with 651 playoff or non-playoff outcomes" (p.10). The predicted seasons are 1995-96 to 2016-17 (p.9: the 15-season window ending 1994-95 predicts 1995-96, repeated "to the point where the 2016-17 season is predicted"); 9 seasons × 29 teams + 13 × 30 = 651. Corrected: 22 seasons, 1995-96 to 2016-17.
   - "playoff probabilities" means the probability of qualifying for the playoffs (approximated as top-eight in conference by win-loss record, p.10), forecast before the season from 10,000 simulations of the 82-game regular season. The paper does not simulate or evaluate playoff series or championship probabilities at all. Corrected.
2. Notes line 124: "naive Monte Carlo playoff probabilities are systematically overconfident across 20 seasons unless game-to-game error correlation is modeled". Partly confirmed: the fix does induce game-to-game correlation, but it is implemented as a season-persistent player-level error (below), and the object is playoff qualification. 22 seasons.
3. Notes line 140: "the documented failure mode is not low per-game accuracy but overconfidence of aggregated series/championship probabilities when rating error is treated as independent across games". Corrected: the paper documents overconfidence of season-level playoff-qualification probabilities when the first-level strength and minutes estimates are treated as known (zero error), not as independent errors; series and championship probabilities are never evaluated.
4. Notes line 145: "No source quantifies the size of the Monte Carlo overconfidence (for example calibration slope or Brier difference)". Corrected: this paper does, see below (p.10–13, Table 6, Figs. 6–9).
5. Report line 60: "this application's exact-bracket computation, which treats a team's series probabilities as independent draws from point-estimate ratings, has exactly this structure". See "Applicability" below; the analogy is structural, not evidential.

### What exactly is over-confident, by how much, over which seasons
- Model: player BPM predicted by linear regression on prior BPM, five-year average BPM, age, prior games played (Eq. 6, p.8); player minutes by regression on prior MP, five-year average MP, age (Eq. 7); team quality = minutes-weighted BPM of the opening-night roster; home-win logistic on home/away predicted BPM, home/away prior-season SRS, home/away days of rest, away-team age (Eq. 8, p.8). Fit on a moving 15-season window; each forecast season out-of-time (p.9).
- The game model is well calibrated (Fig. 4, p.11) with AUC 0.687 on 27,739 games (Fig. 3, p.10). Playoff-qualification ranking is good: AUC 0.903 on 651 team-seasons (Fig. 5, p.11).
- Overconfidence of the naive simulation (point estimates taken as fixed, 10,000 sims per season), p.10–11: 47 teams given 10,000/10,000 to make the playoffs and 29 given 0/10,000, i.e. 76 of 651 team-seasons "certain"; 244 of 651 (37 percent) given below 1 percent or above 99 percent (p.11). In the below-10-percent bin (180 teams) the model predicted 3.7 qualifiers (under 2 percent) and 20 (over 11 percent) qualified, "a factor of 5" (p.11, Fig. 7); every bin above 50 percent is over-estimated (Fig. 7, p.12).
- Size in MSE (Brier) terms, Table 6, p.13, naive vs propagated: 0–10 percent bin 0.10 vs 0.04; 10–20 percent 0.21 vs 0.07; 20–30 percent 0.20 vs 0.15; 80–90 percent 0.31 vs 0.03; 90–100 percent 0.04 vs 0.03; middle bins 30–70 percent unchanged or slightly worse (0.20 vs 0.23; 0.24 vs 0.23; 0.24 vs 0.24; 0.25 vs 0.23). Overall MSE 0.1323 naive vs 0.1288 propagated (p.13), so the gain is almost entirely at the extremes.
- Empirical residual correlation between adjacent games in the out-of-sample game model: 0.04 for home teams (n = 27,391) and 0.043 for away teams (n = 27,436), both positive and "highly significant" but small (p.11).
- Comparison with FiveThirtyEight's published pre-season playoff odds, 2015-16 and 2016-17 (60 team-seasons, Tables 7–8, p.13–14): FiveThirtyEight MSE 5.11 percent and mean absolute deviation 30.4 percent; propagated model 4.25 percent and 25.7 percent; FiveThirtyEight's typical team sits at 78 or 22 percent (average absolute deviation from 50 percent of 28 percent) against 26 percent for the paper's model, and is more confident for 34 of 60 teams (p.14). FiveThirtyEight's own correlation device is updating Elo after each simulated game so "team strength through the season has path dependence" (p.7, p.13).

### What the fix is and how it is parameterised
- Variance propagation (Section 4.4, p.12): for each simulated season, draw one normal error term per player for the BPM model and one for the minutes model, "with means of 0 and standard deviations matching the prediction standard errors from the respective regressions", hold them "constant across the season", and redraw for the next simulated season. The error is therefore perfectly correlated across all 82 games within a simulated season and independent between simulations; it is parameter (estimation) uncertainty, not a fitted autocorrelation of game residuals.
- Correlation across seasons: none; each season is modelled separately.
- The autocorrelation parameter ρ appears only in the two-game illustration (Section 3.2, Eq. 2, p.5: Var((X1+X2)/2) = ((1+ρ)²σ² + σ²)/4) and is not estimated or used in the simulation.
- Result: Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 (p.12–13), extreme bins depopulated, actual/predicted ratios near 100 percent in every bin.

### Applicability to an exact-bracket computation from point-estimate ratings
- The paper separates two things: Monte Carlo sampling error (Section 3.3, Chebyshev bound, p.5, 10,000 draws give under 1 percent error) and model error (Section 3.4: "the Monte Carlo is a garbage-in-garbage-out estimator", p.6; "If our model is overly certain, not only do we have that false certainty, but we may be additionally over-confident through a highly precise Monte Carlo estimate", p.6). An exact bracket computation removes only the first; it is the infinite-simulation limit of the naive simulation and inherits all of the second. So the paper's argument applies to the application's exact bracket in principle: ratings are estimates, and treating them as known understates variance.
- But the evidence in the paper is for pre-season 82-game forecasts where one roster shock (Table 1, p.7: Bird, Robinson, McGrady, Paul, Durant absences cost 20 to 40 wins) moves 82 correlated outcomes; the bracket forecast is made after those 82 games are observed, over at most 28 games, so the shared-error component is smaller in form. The paper offers no measurement of playoff-series or title overconfidence.
- Direction matters: variance propagation flattens probabilities. The report's own numbers (Elo favourite wins 6 of 21, mean champion probability 0.222, favourite mean below FiveThirtyEight's 0.419) show the application's title forecasts are already flat, so this fix would reduce discrimination further; Table 6 shows the propagated model gains only in the sub-30 and over-80 percent bins and loses slightly in the middle, which is where title favourites sit. The honest statement is that the paper identifies a structural defect whose empirical size for a post-season bracket is unmeasured.

### Figures not yet used by the report
- Rest coefficients in the game model, Table 5, p.10: home rest +0.057 (SE 0.019) and away rest −0.0758 (0.019) log-odds per day of rest in the 1995 window; +0.057 (0.017) and −0.0569 (0.017) in the 2016 window. About 10 Elo points per day of rest (my conversion). Away-team age −0.021 to −0.047 per year.
- Game model AUC 0.687 on 27,739 games (Fig. 3, p.10) is a usable benchmark for a pre-season player-aggregate model against the application's per-game Elo.

### Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo championship simulator
Correct citation for "treat rating uncertainty as shared across a team's games rather than as known"; it does not support any statement about series or championship overconfidence, and an exact bracket is affected in the same way as a Monte Carlo one.

## 2. Rump 2006, "The Effects of Home-Away Sequencing on the Length of Best-of-Seven Game Playoff Series"

### Bibliographic facts
- Author: Christopher M. Rump, Bowling Green State University (PDF p.1–2).
- Venue: Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports, Vol. 2, Issue 1, 2006, Article 5, The Berkeley Electronic Press (PDF p.2); repository copy ScholarWorks@BGSU, Applied Statistics and Operations Research Faculty Publications 2, https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/asor_pub/2 (PDF p.1).
- Copyright line, PDF p.2: "Copyright ©2006 The Berkeley Electronic Press. All rights reserved." Repository cover, PDF p.1: "This Article is brought to you for free and open access by the College of Business at ScholarWorks@BGSU. It has been accepted for inclusion in Applied Statistics and Operations Research Faculty Publications by an authorized administrator of ScholarWorks@BGSU." No Creative Commons licence is stated; the file is open on the author's institutional repository, which the README accepts.
- Citation: Rump 2006 is correct (the journal title begins "The Effects of").

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. Notes line 130: a home/away Bernoulli model "is preferable to the simpler, well-studied but ill-fitting binomial model that ignores home-field advantage". Confirmed verbatim, abstract (PDF p.3).
2. Sloan notes line 119: "Rump (2006) home-away Bernoulli model preferred to the binomial for best-of-seven series length". Confirmed: Table 4, p.11, chi-square goodness of fit on 305 NBA series, binomial p = 0.000, 2-2 home-away family p = 0.403; p.12 "the binomial model does not fit the data. On the other hand, the 2-2 home-away model fits the data fairly well".
3. Report line 60: "Within-series effects the literature supports are home-away sequencing". Corrected in scope: under Rump's model (independent games) the sequencing changes only the number of games played, never the probability of winning the series, which "is the same for any format" (p.4, following Bassett and Hurley 1998; Theorem 1, p.4; Theorem 2, p.8). The report should not list sequencing as something that alters series-win probability; the Game 1 figure in the same sentence (NBA.com) is a series-state statistic, not a sequencing effect.

### Figures bearing on the report and the parity profile
- Data: 305 NBA best-of-seven series in the 2-2 format family, 1947 to 2005, 1,744 games, mean length 5.718 (p.10); footnote 4 notes only 24 first-round series are included (format extended to seven games in 2003).
- The favoured (home-court) team won 1,055 of 1,744 games (60.5 percent); at home 706/959 = 0.736, away 349/785 = 0.445 (p.10). Implied home team win rate over all 1,744 games (my computation) = (706 + 436)/1744 = 65.5 percent, about 111 Elo; favourite strength ≈ 70 Elo and home swing ≈ 216 Elo, i.e. ±108 Elo (my conversions of the two Bernoulli rates).
- Series outcomes, Table 4, p.11: 4-0: 33, 4-1: 74, 4-2: 53, 4-3: 71, 3-4: 15, 2-4: 36, 1-4: 14, 0-4: 9. The home-court team won 231 of 305 series = 75.7 percent (my sum); this sits beside the report's Samford 74 percent for 2009–2018.
- Game 7: the home team won 71 of 86 (0.826), above the model's 0.736 (p.12); all models over-predict 3-4 outcomes.
- Series-length distribution, Table 5, p.12: actual 4: 0.138, 5: 0.289, 6: 0.292, 7: 0.282, mean 5.718; 2-2 model 0.129/0.294/0.280/0.297, mean 5.746; binomial 0.158/0.271/0.298/0.273, mean 5.686. Rule of thumb p.12–13: 1 in 7 series is a sweep, 2 in 7 each for 5, 6 and 7 games.
- Switching the 2-2 rounds to 2-3 adds 0.055 games per series, about one extra game per 14-series postseason (p.13); the 25 Finals played in 2-3 format averaged 5.720 games (footnote 8, p.13).
- Section 5, p.14: for the NHL neither the binomial nor the home-away model fits; a two-parameter Markov chain around the home-away model "performs extremely well" (Rump 2005, Chance Magazine), a precursor of the series-state Markov chains in notes line 129.

### Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo championship simulator
Validates a per-game home/away split as necessary for series-length distributions (useful for simulating game counts and venues) and establishes that with independent games the home-away order does not change the series winner, so an exact bracket needs only the per-game probabilities, not the format, to compute who advances.

## 3. Chance 2020, "Conditional probability and the length of a championship series in baseball, basketball, and hockey"

### Bibliographic facts (p.111)
- Author: Don Chance, James C. Flores Endowed Chair of MBA Studies and Professor of Finance, Louisiana State University; dated 17 November 2019.
- Venue: Journal of Sports Analytics 6 (2020) 111–127, DOI 10.3233/JSA-200422, IOS Press.
- Copyright/licence line, p.111: "ISSN 2215-020X/20/$35.00 © 2020 – IOS Press and the authors. All rights reserved" and "This article is published online with Open Access and distributed under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution Non-Commercial License (CC BY-NC 4.0)."
- Citation: Chance 2020 is correct.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. Sloan notes line 119: "a JSA paper finds constant series-win-probability models inconsistent with historical NBA Finals data". Corrected: the model tested is a constant per-game win probability across the series (abstract, p.111), not a series-win probability; and for the NBA it is the sport where the constant model is least rejected: p = 0.5 gives chi-square 0.41, p-value 0.0622 (Table 3, p.116), "Basketball fits the constant probability rule more closely than the other two sports" (p.118), and any constant p from 0.43 to 0.57 fits "virtually the same" (p.117, Fig. 1B). The abstract's "inconsistent with historical data in all three sports" is the author's reading of the conditional frequencies (Table 4), not of the goodness-of-fit test for the NBA.
2. Report line 60 and notes lines 128, 141 attribute negative serial correlation in Finals to JORS 2012, not to this paper. Not in this paper by that name, but Chance's Table 4 (p.119) corroborates the direction: the team up 1-0 wins Game 2 47.06 percent of the time (n = 68), the team up 2-0 wins Game 3 37.50 percent (n = 32), up 2-1 wins Game 4 48.21 percent (n = 56); and the author's conclusion is "there is clearly no momentum in baseball and basketball" (p.122). He attributes the pattern to venue alternation and a "super-home advantage" in critical games rather than to momentum (p.123–124).

### Figures bearing on the report and the parity profile (NBA Finals 1951–2018, 68 series, 392 games)
- Table 2, p.115: home team wins 61.73 percent of Finals games (≈83 Elo home edge, my conversion); the team with more home games wins 66.67 percent of series; the team with the better regular-season record wins 73.44 percent of series (the text on p.114–115 says 72.88 percent; p.122 says "more than 73%"; the table figure is the one to cite). Formats: 31 Finals in 2-3-2, 33 in 2-2-1-1-1, 2 in 1-2-2-1-1, 2 in 1-1-1-1-1-1-1.
- Footnote 9, p.115: Finals in the 2-3-2 format averaged 6.32 games against 5.94 in 2-2-1-1-1, consistent with Rump's prediction.
- Series length, Table 3, p.116: 4 games 13.24 percent, 5: 25.00, 6: 33.82, 7: 27.94; mean 5.76 (p = 0.5 expectation 5.81). Mean-matching constant p = 0.5671; maximum-likelihood p = 0.44/0.56 (p.116–117).
- Conditional frequencies, Table 4, p.119: ahead 3-0 wins Game 4 75.00 percent (n = 12); ahead 3-1 wins Game 5 56.67 (60); ahead 3-2 wins Game 6 54.76 (42). Teams down 3-0 have won Game 4 only 25 percent; the series has never been reversed.
- Home conditional frequencies, Table 7, p.124: Game 1 home team wins 76.47 percent (68); Game 7 home team wins 84.21 percent (19); tied 2-2, home team wins Game 5 68.97 percent (29); behind 1-0 at home wins Game 2 83.33 percent (18); ahead 1-0 at home wins Game 2 58.00 percent (50).
- Regular-season home win rates quoted for comparison, p.123: NBA 59.5 percent (Jamieson 2010) and 55.7 percent (Moskowitz and Wertheim 2012), "extremely close" to the Finals figure.
- Section 7.3, p.125: an "evenly matched series" is not the same as a series of evenly matched games; a series of 50-50 games does not maximise the chance of seven games unless the trailing team's conditional probability exceeds 0.5.

### Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo championship simulator
Supplies the Finals base rates for the parity profile (better-record team 73 percent, home team 62 percent of games) and shows that a constant per-game probability is adequate for NBA series length; series-state conditioning adds little for the NBA, so an i.i.d. per-game model with a home term is defensible.

## 4. Bowman, Harmon and Ashman 2023, "Schedule inequity in the National Basketball Association"

### Bibliographic facts (p.61)
- Authors: R. Alan Bowman (Reh School of Business, Clarkson University), Oskar Harmon (University of Connecticut, corresponding), Thomas Ashman (Eckerd College).
- Venue: Journal of Sports Analytics 9 (2023) 61–76, DOI 10.3233/JSA-220629, IOS Press. Received 4 February 2022, accepted 24 August 2022, pre-press 20 December 2022, published 23 March 2023.
- Copyright/licence line, p.61: "ISSN 2215-020X © 2023 – The authors. Published by IOS Press. This is an Open Access article distributed under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial License (CC BY-NC 4.0)."
- Citation: Bowman, Harmon, Ashman 2023 is correct.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. Sloan notes line 99: "Bowman, Harmon and Ashman (JSA 2023) find a visiting team on a back-to-back against a rested home team shifts win probability over 2000-01 to 2018-19". Confirmed in substance: seasons 2000-01 to 2018-19 (abstract, p.61; p.64); logistic regression with team dummies as controls; reference category is two days since the previous game for both teams (p.65); visitor one day since last game multiplies the home team's win odds by 1.506 (p < 0.0001) and home team one day since last game by 0.806 (p < 0.0001) (Table 1, p.66). Average effect on home win probability per occurrence: visitor back-to-back 0.0774, home back-to-back 0.0412 (Table 2, column 2, p.67).
2. The "about 44 percent" back-to-back win rate in the same note line belongs to the arXiv paper, not to this one. Not in the paper.

### Figures bearing on the report and the parity profile
- Odds multipliers, Table 1, p.66, with my conversions to Elo points and to the shift at a 0.60 home baseline: visitor back-to-back 1.506 (+71 Elo, +9 pp); home back-to-back 0.806 (−38 Elo, −5 pp); visitor three or more games in the last week 1.153 (+25 Elo, +3 pp, p = 0.005); visitor travel over 1,000 miles if back-to-back or over 2,000 if not 1.261 (+40 Elo, +5 pp, p = 0.004); home four or more games in the last week 0.914 (−16 Elo, −2 pp, p = 0.011); home travelled one time zone west to east the day before 0.693 (−64 Elo, −9 pp, p = 0.001); two or more zones two days before 0.833 (−32 Elo, p = 0.11); visitor more than six days off 1.741 (+96 Elo, p = 0.03, n = 118); visitor five or six days off 0.771 (p = 0.12).
- Back-to-backs account for 44.1 percent of schedule imbalance, travel 16.4 percent, compressed schedules 15.3 percent; effects on the visiting team are 59.3 percent of the total against 40.7 percent on the home team (p.66–67, Fig. 1).
- Size for team success, averaged over 19 seasons (Tables 4–5, p.70; p.64, p.75): range between most and least favoured teams of 1.87 expected wins per season (average absolute value 0.36 wins); range in playoff probability 0.0825 (AAV 0.0085); range in championship probability 0.0065 (AAV 0.00033; the largest season 0.0269 in 2004); range in league rank 0.99 places.
- Trend, p.72–73, Fig. 3: visitor back-to-backs fell from about 35 percent of games (2000) to 20 percent (2018), home back-to-backs from about 15 to 12 percent; slopes −0.513 and −0.195 percentage points per year (p = 0.0005 and < 0.0001); all inequity measures trend down.
- Literature cited, p.63: Entine and Small (2008) found a home scoring-margin advantage of 3.24 points in 2004–2006 "mostly attributable to the visiting team playing more back-to-back games (10.4%)"; Ashman et al. (2010) found the home team's win rate against the spread fell from 50.63 to 45.86 percent when the home team had no rest and the visitor did (1990–2009); Huyghe et al. (2018): eastward-travelling teams score more and win more. NBA teams averaged 43,534 miles of travel in 2018-19, 7 percent more than the NHL (p.64).

### Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo championship simulator
Rest and travel move single-game probabilities by 2 to 9 percentage points (25 to 70 Elo), larger than the report's debated 30-Elo home-term error, but net out to under one percentage point of title probability per team, so the parity profile can ignore schedule effects while a per-game Elo should not. Playoff games have no back-to-backs, so any part of a regular-season home advantage that is really visitor fatigue (Entine and Small's reading) does not carry into the playoffs; this is a candidate explanation for the report's finding that the fixed home term bites hardest in the playoffs (inference, not in the paper).
