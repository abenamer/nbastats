# Verification of the six benchmark and predictability papers against their full text

Scope: research/basketball-simulation-accuracy-gap/report.md and notes/*.md (mainly elo_and_market_benchmarks.md, player_level_and_ml_models.md, sloan_papers.md) checked against local copies of the papers (read on 2026-10-23). Every page of each PDF was read (text extraction plus page images where the tool returned them). Page numbers below are PDF page numbers unless marked "journal p."

Verdict key: CONFIRMED (page) / CORRECTED (what the paper says, page) / NOT IN THE PAPER.

## 1. Aoki, Assunção, Vaz de Melo 2017, "Luck is Hard to Beat: The Difficulty of Sports Prediction"

### Bibliographic facts (from the PDF)
- Authors: Raquel Aoki, Renato Assunção, Pedro Vaz de Melo (Department of Computer Science, UFMG, Belo Horizonte, Brazil) (p.1).
- Year and venue: KDD '17, Halifax, NS, Canada (p.1 footer). The file is the arXiv preprint arXiv:1706.02447v1 [cs.LG], 8 Jun 2017 (p.1 margin). 10 pages.
- Licence or copyright line on the first page: yes, the ACM permission block: "Permission to make digital or hard copies of all or part of this work for personal or classroom use is granted without fee provided that copies are not made or distributed for profit or commercial advantage ... To copy otherwise, or republish, to post on servers or to redistribute to lists, requires prior specific permission and/or a fee. Request permissions from permissions@acm.org. KDD '17, Halifax, NS, Canada © 2017 ACM. 978-x-xxxx-xxxx-x/YY/MM. . . $15.00 DOI: 10.1145/nnnnnnn.nnnnnnn" (p.1). No Creative Commons licence. The README rule ("arXiv's default licence does not allow third-party redistribution ... ACM: link only") therefore does not clear this file for the repository.
- Citation: Aoki, Assunção, Vaz de Melo 2017 is correct.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 75 and player_level_and_ml_models.md line 45, report.md line 34: "198 leagues, 1,503 seasons, 4 sports" — CONFIRMED (abstract p.1; p.5: 270,713 matches, January 2007 to July 2016, 84 countries; basketball 42 leagues / 310 seasons).
2. "the NBA underdog wins with probability about 0.36, stable across seasons, interpreted by the authors as the luck component" — CONFIRMED (abstract p.1 "0.36 the probability that an underdog team wins in the NBA league"; p.8 section 5.3 "It is stable over the seasons and approximately equal to 0.36. This is the component of luck in the NBA outcomes"; Table 2 p.8: P(U) 0.35, 0.36, 0.37, 0.37, 0.34 for 2012-2016, mean 0.36). Note the conclusions (p.9) round it to "35% an underdog team wins a match" and "about 35% of the times in NBA". Note also the scope: the underdog is defined by the paper's own skill estimates α fitted to the same regular seasons (2012-2016, 5 seasons), so 0.64 favourite accuracy is a hindsight skill ordering, not an out-of-sample forecast.
3. "home advantage adds about 0.09" — CORRECTED. The abstract (p.1) says "with a home advantage adding 0.09 to this probability", which is 0.45 (underdog at home) minus the unconditional 0.36. The body states the home-versus-away swing: Table 2 p.8 gives P(U|away) 0.27 and P(U|home) 0.45, and the text says "Playing at home rather than away increases the underdog probability in approximately 0.45 − 0.27 = 0.18, a substantial advantage" (p.8); the conclusions repeat "the home advantage of an underdog team adds 0.18 with respect to the average probability of 0.27 when it plays away from home" (p.9). So: underdog wins 27% away, 45% at home; 0.09 is only the difference from the unconditional 0.36.
4. "the skill coefficient φ for the NBA is above 0.95" — CONFIRMED (p.6: "For NBA, ϕ is always above 0.95").
5. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 75: "the authors conclude 'there are no algorithms capable of producing accurate predictions and there is some evidence they will never be found'" — CORRECTED. The sentence is in the introduction (p.1), not a conclusion, and it cites Martin et al. 2016 [15]: "Despite the large amount of money involved, there are no algorithms capable of producing accurate predictions and there is some evidence they will never be found [15]." The paper's own closing message is "sometimes the culprit is luck, about 35% of the times in NBA. It is hard to beat luck in sports." (p.9).
6. Quote "luck is substantially present even in the most competitive championships, which partially explains why sophisticated and complex feature-based models hardly beat simple models" — CONFIRMED verbatim (abstract p.1; the sentence ends "in the task of forecasting sports' outcomes").
7. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 76: "up to 62% of NBA teams would need removing before the league's final ranking became random" — CONFIRMED (p.6: "requiring the removal of up to 62% of the teams to make it random"; also "we need to remove between 17 to 25 teams out of 30").
8. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 134: "Favorite (model-defined) wins ≈64% of NBA games, underdog ≈36%" — CONFIRMED as arithmetic on P(U) = 0.36 (p.8).
9. report.md line 34 "reach the same figure from a non-market direction" — CONFIRMED in substance: skills come from a Bradley-Terry/Poisson graphical model on scores plus roster and salary features (pp.4-5, 7), not from betting odds.

### Figures in the paper that the report does not yet use
- Related work (p.2): Ben-Naim, Vazquez and Redner's upset probability q ≈ 0.35 for basketball and football, ≈ 0.45 for soccer and baseball; Pelechrinis's PageRank NBA ranking "has an accuracy of about 67%"; introduction (p.1) cites Blastland and Dilnot: bettors' favourites win 70% of the time in football and basketball.
- Underdog probability when the favourite is one of the dominant teams removed by the ϕ procedure: P(U|away, R+) 0.19 and P(U|home, R+) 0.17, "The home advantage almost disappears in this case" (Table 2 and text, p.8).
- Correlation between estimated skill and regular-season wins averages 0.7399 over 2012-2016 (p.8); the 2016 champion had the 4th-highest skill estimate and 4th-most wins (p.8).
- All basketball leagues in the sample have a significant skill component; basketball needs about 50% of teams removed to become random versus 19-20% for soccer (p.6, p.8).
- Features that raise skill: average of the top-5 salaries and average PER positive; roster size negative; roster coherence positive except 2015 (p.8).

### Relevance to an Elo-based Monte Carlo championship simulator
Even with a hindsight ordering of team skill the weaker NBA team wins about 36% of regular-season games (27% away, 45% at home), which bounds any game-level accuracy near 64-68% and is the right reference for the simulator's 66%; the 0.18 home swing is a direct empirical check for the home-court term.

## 2. Lopez, Matthews, Baumer 2017, "How often does the best team win? A unified approach to understanding randomness in North American sport"

### Bibliographic facts (from the PDF)
- Authors: Michael J. Lopez (Skidmore College), Gregory J. Matthews (Loyola University Chicago), Benjamin S. Baumer (Smith College) (p.1).
- Year and venue: the file is arXiv:1701.05976v3 [stat.AP], 22 Nov 2017, headed "Submitted to the Annals of Applied Statistics" (p.1); footer "imsart-aoas ... date: November 23, 2017". The AoAS 2018 publication is not stated in the PDF. 47 pages (main text pp.1-34, supplement pp.35-47).
- Licence or copyright line on the first page: none. (p.11 mentions only that the betting-market data cannot be shared "due to licensing restrictions"; code and posterior draws are at github.com/bigfour/competitiveness.)
- Citation: Lopez, Matthews, Baumer 2017 is correct for the 2017 arXiv version; the notes cite it as "Annals of Applied Statistics 2018", so either year is defensible.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. "Bayesian state-space models fit to betting-market probabilities for NFL, NHL, NBA, MLB over about a decade" — CONFIRMED (abstract p.1; p.7: 2006-2016 seasons, Sports Insights closing lines, NBA 13,290 games with 13,245 lines; p.8: 11 NBA seasons, 25 weeks each).
2. "the NBA shows 'both the largest dispersion in talent and the largest home advantage'" — CONFIRMED verbatim (abstract p.1).
3. "the best team's median probability of winning a neutral-site game is highest in the NBA at about 67% (NFL ~64%, NHL ~57%, MLB ~56%)" — CONFIRMED as worded (p.25: "The median probability of the best team winning a neutral site game is highest in the NBA (67%), followed in order by the NFL (64%), NHL (57%), and MLB (56%)"), but the meaning needs stating: "best team" is the better of two randomly drawn teams in a randomly drawn week, "the one, a priori, with a higher probability of winning that game" (p.24, simulation steps p.25), not the league's best team. The report's "the best team's median probability" should say "the better of two randomly chosen teams".
4. "about 27% of NBA neutral-site matchups give the best team odds above 3:1" — CONFIRMED (p.25: "roughly 27% of NBA and 20% of NFL neutral site match-ups are greater than this 3:1 threshold").
5. "when the best NBA team plays at home it is always favored at ≥60%, with the middle half of games between 68% and 84%" — CONFIRMED (p.25).
6. "a typical NBA home team has a 62.0% chance against an equal opponent (range 60% Brooklyn to 66.1% Denver), vs 58.9% NFL, 55.5% NHL, 54% MLB" — CONFIRMED (p.20: MLB 54.0%, NHL 55.5%, NFL 58.9%, NBA 62.0%; Brooklyn 60%, Denver 66.1%; p.29 restates 62.0%).
7. sloan_papers.md line 98: single games reveal little because "team i will typically only play team j once at time k" — CONFIRMED verbatim (p.2).
8. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 79: "The paper notes its 'better team = higher pre-game probability' framing is 'ideal for measuring league parity looking backwards' but 'less appropriate to make future game predictions'" — CORRECTED. The quoted words (p.11) are about the cumulative fits of Models IHA and CHA, which "blend outcomes from weeks prior to, during, and after week k to estimate team strength. While this is ideal for measuring league parity looking backwards, it is less appropriate to make future game predictions." They do not refer to the better-team framing; the paper's answer is the sequential fits of Section 5.4.
9. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md lines 78, 93, 127, 136, 148: "Better team wins a best-of-seven NBA series about 80% of the time" (from a Complexity Digest summary) — NOT IN THE PAPER. The paper never reports a series win probability. Its postseason results are a pseudo-R-squared parity metric: PostParity16 = 0.43 for the NBA and 0.51 NFL versus 0.88 MLB and 0.85 NHL for 16-team, best-of-7 tournaments (p.28), Figure 8 (p.29) and Figure 20 (p.47).
10. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 78: "matching that would require best-of-11 in the NFL, best-of-51 in the NHL and best-of-75 in MLB" — CORRECTED. The text states only that "MLB would have to switch to a 16-team tournament in which each round was approximately 75-game series" and that "the NBA would have to reduce the number of playoff teams to 8, and play a single-game tournament" to match the other three (p.28). No best-of-11 or best-of-51 figure appears in the text (Figure 8 plots series lengths 1-75 with ticks at 11 and 51, but no values are stated).
11. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 148: "the series-level 80% figure is a model estimate (Lopez et al.) rather than a raw count" — NOT IN THE PAPER (see 9).
12. sloan_papers.md line 57: "A later Loyola paper reports an AUC of 0.756 for NBA games and says mid-season won-loss ratio and point differential are about as good as its strength estimates, 'confirming the findings of the Wolfson group'" — CORRECTED. The "Loyola paper" is this paper (the eCommons copy is Matthews's institutional deposit). Table 5 (p.24) gives NBA AUC 0.756 for both the observed market probabilities and the sequential model fits; p.22 says "by the NBA's mid-season, won-loss ratio and point differential are similar to our estimates of team strength in assessing future performance. By and large, this confirms the findings of Wolfson and Koopmeiners (2015)". The words "confirming the findings of the Wolfson group" are not in the paper.

### Figures in the paper that the report does not yet use
- Market calibration with a peer-reviewed source: Hosmer-Lemeshow tests in 10 bins "did not show evidence of a lack of fit" in any of the four leagues, so the authors "interpret these probabilities as 'true'" (pp.7-8, Figure 1 p.9). Table 1 (p.7): NBA home win rate 0.595 observed against 0.615 mean implied, average vig 1.93%.
- Table 5 (p.24): NBA AUC 0.756 and Brier 0.194 (p-value 0.803) for closing-market probabilities, and AUC 0.756 and Brier 0.194 (p 0.759) for the sequential state-space fits, in the last season of the data (2015-16); the authors say sequential fits "can closely approximate the observed game probabilities" and that there is no arbitrage. This is a published market-vs-model Brier for the NBA, albeit for one season and for a model trained on market probabilities.
- Table 3 (p.16): NBA team strength on the logit scale has sd 0.660, 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles -1.27 to 1.20 (a strength of 1.0 logit is a 73.1% chance against an average team at a neutral site, p.14); spread 3.6 times MLB's. Worst team ever: 2007-08 Miami Heat at -2.2 logit (p.16).
- Table 4 (p.18): NBA γ_season 0.618 (38% reversion to the mean between seasons, p.19), γ_week 0.977, σ_game 0.274 (the largest game-level error of the four leagues), σ_season 0.44, σ_week 0.166; the NBA has the highest week-to-week uncertainty, which the authors attribute to injuries, resting of starters and in-season trades (p.18).
- Model IHA (team-specific home advantage) beats constant home advantage by DIC 324 in the NBA, the largest improvement of the four leagues (Table 2 p.12); Denver's home advantage is worth about 1.68 extra wins per season over an average team and 2.5 over Brooklyn, with the caveat that the model ignores lineups and injuries (p.20).
- Regular-season parity: RegParity NBA 0.66 at a neutral site and 0.47 when the better team is at home (0 = every game predetermined, 1 = all coin flips) (p.27).
- Figure 6 (p.23): R-squared between NBA sequential strength estimates and future win percentage reaches 60-80% by about game 20 and is matched by point differential and won-loss by mid-season.

### Relevance to an Elo-based Monte Carlo championship simulator
The paper supplies published NBA scale parameters (between-season reversion 38%, team-strength sd 0.66 logit, home edge 62%) and a one-season Brier of 0.194 for the closing market that a backtest can be compared with, and its 16-team best-of-7 simulations give a parity benchmark (PostParity 0.43) for the playoff bracket, but it gives no series-level win rate, so the 80% figure must go or be re-sourced.

## 3. Zimmermann 2015, "Exploring chance in NCAA basketball"

### Bibliographic facts (from the PDF)
- Author: Albrecht Zimmermann, INSA Lyon (p.1).
- Year and venue: arXiv:1508.04688v1 [stat.AP], 19 Aug 2015 (p.1 margin). No conference or journal named. 12 pages.
- Licence or copyright line on the first page: none.
- Citation: Zimmermann 2015 is correct.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 82: "simulation-based ceiling estimates that assume simple noise models are too optimistic; season-specific limits derived from team-profile clustering and schedule give 'higher assessments of chance and tighter limits on predictive accuracy'" — CONFIRMED (abstract p.1; summary p.10).
2. The 74-75% "glass ceiling" (report.md line 34, notes lines 80, 92, player_level line 44): NCAA or NBA? — NCAA. This paper's introduction (p.1) says: "In our last work on the topic of NCAA basketball [7], we speculated about the existence of a 'glass ceiling' in (semi-)professional sports match outcome prediction, noting that season-long accuracies in the mid-seventies seemed to be the best that could be achieved for college basketball, with similar results for other sports." The NBA is not analysed; it is named only as future work: "We would expect similar effects in the NFL, and even in the NBA, where conference membership has an effect" (p.10). The report's statement that the ceiling "comes from Zimmermann and co-authors on college basketball" is CONFIRMED; the explanation "where talent dispersion is far wider" is the report's own reasoning and is NOT IN THE PAPER (the paper attributes the ceiling to chance and to missing attributes such as "player experience or synergies", p.1).
3. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 80: "the limit is attributed either to missing attributes (experience, synergies) or to 'a large amount of chance in outcomes'" (attributed to the 2013 paper) — CONFIRMED in substance by this paper's p.1 for the two explanations; "multi-layer perceptrons performed best" is NOT IN THIS PAPER (it is a claim about arXiv 1310.3607, which is not in the repository).
4. player_level_and_ml_models.md line 44: the companion "Chance and the predictive limit in basketball" paper "states similar results hold for the NBA" — NOT IN THIS PAPER (this 2015 paper says the NBA is future work, p.10; the Springer chapter cited in the notes is not in the repository).
5. player_level_and_ml_models.md line 66: "Exact numbers for the Zimmermann NBA predictive limit ... were not retrievable" — this paper has no NBA numbers; it has NCAA numbers (next section).

### Figures in the paper that the report does not yet use
- Table 4 (p.8): season-specific NCAA Division I limits on predictive accuracy, 2008-2013: 0.7132, 0.7329, 0.7467, 0.7329, 0.7257, 0.7339 (unconstrained EM clustering), or 0.7610, 0.7329, 0.7648, 0.7329, 0.7573, 0.7345 (KS-optimised clustering); estimated share of games decided by chance 0.47-0.57. KenPom's Log5/Pythagorean prediction scored 0.7035-0.7307 on the same seasons, "always close to the limit and in one case (2012) actually exceeds it" (p.8). The limits "stay significantly below 80%" (p.9).
- Method: limit = (1 − c) + c/2 where c is the share of games decided by chance (p.3); Burke's NFL c = 0.525 gives 0.7375 and Weissbock's NHL c = 0.76 gives 0.62 (pp.2-3).
- In the cluster re-encoded 2008 schedule, home court pushes same-cluster win rates "always above 60%" (p.7).
- Caveat stated by the author: models with extra information (player experience, network comparisons) "can exceed the limits reported in Table 4" (p.10); training on previous seasons can bias a learner when the seasons differ in chance (p.10).
- The author cites Oh, Keshri and Iyengar's Sloan 2015 simulation as "particularly inspirational" for exploring counterfactual NBA playoff series (p.10).

### Relevance to an Elo-based Monte Carlo championship simulator
The glass-ceiling figure the report quotes is a college-basketball number from a method that reports season-specific limits of 71-76%, so it should not be used as an NBA ceiling; the paper's method (re-encode a season's schedule by team cluster, read chance off the loss rate of the stronger cluster) could be applied to the NBA backtest to give a season-specific ceiling instead of the market proxy.

## 4. Bunker and Susnjak 2019, "The Application of Machine Learning Techniques for Predicting Results in Team Sport: A Review"

### Bibliographic facts (from the PDF)
- Authors: Rory Bunker (Nagoya Institute of Technology, Japan; corresponding author), Teo Susnjak (Massey University, New Zealand) (p.1).
- Year and venue: arXiv:1912.11762v1 [cs.LG], 26 Dec 2019; "Preprint submitted to arXiv December 30, 2019" (p.1). No journal named. 48 pages. Covers 31 papers, 1996-2019, nine sports (p.4).
- Licence or copyright line on the first page: none.
- Citation: Bunker, Susnjak 2019 is correct (the title begins "The Application of Machine Learning Techniques for...").

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. report.md line 34 and player_level_and_ml_models.md line 43: "Loeffelholz, Bednar and Bauer's 74.33 percent neural network on roughly 620-650 games of 2007-08" and "versus USA Today experts at 68.67%" — CONFIRMED with precision: Bunker gives 74.3% and 68.7% (p.12), 650 matches in Table 2 (p.9); the text explains 620 matches were used for training and testing and 30 held out as "un-played" validation games (p.12), so 620 and 650 are both right (the 74.33 and 68.67 decimals come from Migliorati p.4, see paper 5). Venue of Loeffelholz et al.: Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports 5 (2009) (reference list p.45).
2. The "65 to 72 percent band" (sloan_papers.md line 75 "the 65-72% ceiling reported in the broader literature"; player_level line 40 "cluster at 65-74%") — NOT IN THE PAPER as a stated band. Bunker's basketball table (Table 2, p.9) lists for the NBA: Miljković et al. 2010, 67% (778 games of 2009-10, Naive Bayes, 10-fold CV, p.14); Cao 2012, 67.8% (about 4,000 games 2005-06 to 2010-11, simple logistic regression; teamrankings.com experts 69.6%, p.16); Zdravevski and Kulakov 2009, 72.8% (1,230 games, two seasons, logistic regression in WEKA, p.12); Loeffelholz et al. 2009, 74.3% (p.12); Thabtah et al. 2019, 83% (430 NBA Finals games 1980-2017, LMT decision tree, 21 features, p.23-24). So the NBA regular-season range in this survey is 67-74%, and the 83% is on Finals games only.
3. Leakage warnings — CONFIRMED but not cited to Bunker anywhere in the report or notes (the report cites a 2025 BRACIS study instead). Bunker pp.32-33: "Cross validation (CV) was found to have been used in a number of prior studies. In the sporting domain this can be problematic if the technique is not appropriately modified. CV randomly shuffles instances, meaning that future games might be used to predict past games, and this pitfall was encountered in a number of studies whose predictive accuracy might have been compromised as a consequence." Conclusions p.41: "Since k-fold cross-validation shuffles instances randomly, it should be altered in a manner that generates order-preserved folds, so as to prevent future matches being used to predict past matches, e.g., using a season-by-season or round-by-round training-validation split." Footnote 6 (p.37): Hubáček et al. 2019a "pointed out that if the purpose of the model is to generate profit through betting strategies, the betting odds should not be included as a model predictor". player_level_and_ml_models.md line 67 ("No NBA-specific published critique of leakage in box-score-based prediction papers was found") is therefore partly superseded: Bunker's critique is sport-general but covers the basketball studies in its tables.

### Figures in the paper that the report does not yet use
- Recommended baselines for any accuracy claim (p.37): the betting-odds favourite, expert predictions, the majority class (home win) and a random pick; "model predictions can be compared to predictions from betting odds, expert predictions, and baselines" (p.41).
- Shi, Moorthy and Zimmermann 2013 on NCAAB (32,236 matches): "experienced an upper limit of 74% accuracy which they could not improve beyond" (p.19) — the origin of the 74% glass-ceiling figure, confirmed as college basketball.
- Valero 2016 (MLB, 24,300 games): model "not significantly more accurate than predictions derived from match betting odds" (p.22); David et al. 2011 (NFL) "comparable to the predictions of bookmakers" (p.15).
- Size does not buy accuracy: in the 2017 Soccer Prediction Challenge with 216,000 matches "all of the studies seemed to obtain accuracies that levelled off at a certain point" (p.27, p.31); feature engineering and selection are "key drivers" (p.31); nearly 90% of papers list richer features as future work (p.38).
- Figure 4 (p.28): highest reported accuracy by sport, basketball 83% (decision tree, the Finals-only study); Figure 6 (p.31): basketball accuracy range across studies about 16 points; Figure 7 (p.32): basketball datasets 430-4,000 matches.
- Caveat for cross-study comparison: studies differ in sport, data set, predictors, class label and seasons, so "there is a limit to the reliability of directly comparing and drawing conclusions from these differences" (p.40).

### Relevance to an Elo-based Monte Carlo championship simulator
Bunker is the source to cite for two things the report asserts from weaker sources: NBA machine-learning accuracies on regular-season games sit at 67-74% with expert and market baselines at 69-70%, and shuffled cross-validation leaks future games into the training set, which is the check to apply before taking any published accuracy above the simulator's as a target.

## 5. Migliorati 2021, "Features selection in NBA outcome prediction through Deep Learning"

### Bibliographic facts (from the PDF)
- Author: Manlio Migliorati, University of Brescia, Italy (p.1).
- Year and venue: arXiv:2111.09695v1 [cs.LG], 17 Nov 2021 (p.1 margin). No journal or conference stated (the layout is a Springer chapter template). 29 pages.
- Licence or copyright line on the first page: none.
- Citation: Migliorati 2021 is correct.

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. player_level_and_ml_models.md line 22: single-feature models using Elo or relative victory frequency "achieve a better quality of fit than models built on box-score predictors such as the Four Factors" — CORRECTED wording. The abstract (p.1) reads: "It is shown how models based on one a single feature (Elo rating or the relative victory frequency) have a quality of fit better than models using box-score predictors (e.g. the Four Factors)." The quoted phrase is a paraphrase and should not be in quotation marks.
2. "deep-learning models with cross-validation over 16 NBA regular seasons" (notes line 22, 56; report.md line 36 "Migliorati's cross-validated deep-learning study over 16 seasons") — CONFIRMED (abstract p.1; p.8: regular seasons 2004-05 to 2019-20 up to 11 March 2020; p.9: 19,138 matches, about 18,000 after removing rows with missing features; p.17: 4-fold cross-validation with a random 75/25 split; footnote 6 p.17: a time-based split, first 14 seasons for training and last 2 for testing, "producing similar results").
3. "features were 'ex ante calculated'" (notes line 56) — CONFIRMED (abstract p.1 "ex ante calculated"; p.2 and p.9 "ex-ante, i.e. considering only information about prior matches").
4. "single-feature Elo beating Four Factors over 16 seasons" — CONFIRMED, with numbers now available (notes line 66 said they were not retrievable): best Elo model (dynamic Elo, averaging depth 2, no home/away split) AUC 0.7117 and accuracy 0.6736 at threshold 0.5049 (p.18, Table 6 p.19, p.23); historical Elo AUC 0.7117, accuracy 0.6721 with 20% regression to the mean, home-advantage parameter 40 and K = 30 (p.18); best diff model AUC 0.7020, accuracy 0.663 (Table 7 p.20); best Four Factors model AUC 0.6655, accuracy 0.6400 (Table 8 p.22). Per season (Table 9 p.23) Elo is best in 9 of 15 seasons, diff in 5, Four Factors in 2; best single season 0.7053 (Elo, 2014-15); Elo's worst 0.6333 (partial 2019-20) and 0.6420 (2008-09). Conclusion (p.22): "comparable to results reported in the literature (with an accuracy about 0.67%-70%)" [sic].

### Figures in the paper that the report does not yet use
- Home-court parameter: the best Elo fit used a home-advantage penalty of 40 Elo points (p.18), against FiveThirtyEight's 100 noted in footnote 5 (p.13); Table 4 (p.13) shows 100 points implies a 0.64 home win probability for equal teams and 50 points 0.57. This independently supports the report's home-court finding.
- Observed NBA home win rate 59.27% over 2004-05 to 2019-20, by season 60.43% (2004-05) down to 57.48% (2014-15), 57.89% (2017-18), 59.27% (2018-19) and 55.10% (partial 2019-20) (Table 3 p.12).
- Regression to the mean matters: historical features without it give accuracy "really low (about 0.615)" versus about 0.66 with 70-90% regression (Fig. 8 p.20, Fig. 10 p.22); Elo initial rating 1300 following Silver 2015 (p.6).
- Network depth adds nothing: 1 hidden layer AUC 0.717 / accuracy 0.6721 versus 10 hidden layers AUC 0.716 / 0.6716 (p.16).
- Literature table (p.4): Loeffelholz 74.33% on 650 games versus experts 68.67%; Miljkovic 67% on 778 games with 141 features; Cao about 70%; Beckler et al. 73% on 1991-92 to 1996-97; Cheng et al. 74.4% on playoff games 2007-08 to 2014-15 (maximum entropy); teamrankings 74.3% for 2017-18 from March games only. The author warns of "a great difficulty in comparing outcomes predictions related to so different dataset" (p.4).
- The author's list of omitted information for Elo: injuries, logistics/altitude, rest days, referees (p.14), and the ~300 in-season transactions per season that break the "homogeneous team" assumption (p.8).

### Relevance to an Elo-based Monte Carlo championship simulator
A deep net fed a single ex-ante Elo feature reaches 67.4% accuracy and AUC 0.71 over 16 seasons, almost exactly the simulator's 66.0%, with the best home-advantage parameter at 40 Elo rather than 100, so the paper is both an external replication of the simulator's game-level accuracy and direct evidence for shrinking its home-court term.

## 6. Wolfson, Koopmeiners, DiLernia 2018, "Who's good this year? Comparing the information content of games in the four major US sports"

### Claims attributed to this paper
1. "the NBA was the most predictable of the four major leagues" — CONFIRMED (p.5: "The NBA was the most predictable of the four major North American professional sports leagues"; p.7).
2. "over 65 percent accuracy using 87.5 percent of games for training" — CONFIRMED verbatim (p.5: "Using 87.5% of games as a training set, our model was able to predict with over 65% accuracy across seasons"). Seasons: 2010-11 to 2015-16, regular season only (p.2). Models: Bradley-Terry logistic regression with a home-advantage term, and a margin-of-victory linear model (p.3). Own arithmetic from the pooled quartic in Table A1 (p.11): the NBA margin-of-victory curve gives 0.644 at 10 games per team, 0.681 at 21, 0.684 at 25, 0.685 at 41 and about 0.705 at 72 games (87.5% of 82), so the pooled plateau is about 68%, with the quartic less reliable at the ends; treat as [computed].
3. "home teams won about 60 percent of NBA games, the largest home edge of the four leagues" — CONFIRMED (p.4: "approximately 60% of NBA games"; p.5: "The NBA also had the largest home court advantage with home teams winning approximately 60% of games").
4. "information levels off at about 25 games" (report.md lines 9 and 62; sloan_papers.md lines 57, 86, 93, 102) — CONFIRMED with a wording caveat. p.5: "model accuracy for the NBA did not improve substantially once more than 25% of games (i.e., ≥ 21 games per team) were included in the training set", with a segmented-regression inflection "when approximately 25-30 games per team were used in the model"; p.7: "our information metric for the NBA appears to plateau at around 25 games". The games are sampled at random from the season, not taken in schedule order: "Our approach therefore does not directly describe how predictive accuracy improves as games are played in their scheduled order" (p.10). So the report's "levels off at about 25 games into the season" should read "about 25 games per team (randomly sampled, not the first 25 of the schedule)".
5. "in the first 5-10 games each added game improves accuracy by roughly 1 percentage point" — CONFIRMED (p.6: "The initial 5-10 games of the NBA season offer more modest, but still substantial, improvements of approximately 1% accuracy per game"; NFL 3-4% per game after the first 2-3 games; NHL below 0.5%, MLB below 0.2%).
6. elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 85 (cited to the arXiv version): "Basketball's long sequence of scoring events makes it harder for the weaker team to win by luck" — CONFIRMED in substance (p.8: "a large number of baskets are scored, and the Law of Large Numbers dictates that each team approaches their 'true' ability level more closely"; NHL goals "strongly influenced by chance", p.9).
7. sloan_papers.md line 102 draws from this paper that "season-long Elo updating adds little discriminating information after the first third of the season" — this is an inference, not a claim in the paper; the paper's models are static within-season Bradley-Terry fits, and the authors note that "more sophisticated models ... might change our conclusions, particularly for sports like the NBA where the predictiveness curve plateaus early" (p.9).
8. report.md line 9 "Wolfson and Koopmeiners' 'Who's good this year?'" — CORRECTED: the JSA 2018 version has three authors (Wolfson, Koopmeiners and DiLernia); the notes already say so.

### Figures in the paper that the report does not yet use
- Margin of victory adds nothing for the NBA: "There was virtually no advantage in including margin of victory in our models" (p.5), whereas it helped slightly in the NFL (p.5). Relevant to the Elo margin-of-victory multiplier.
- Raw playoff upset counts for six seasons: lower-seeded teams won 27.1% of first-round NBA series (2010-11 to 2015-16), versus 25.0% of NFL divisional-round games, 43.8% of NHL first-round series and 58.3% in MLB (pp.7-8). This is a raw count of higher-seed series wins (72.9%) that elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 148 says no source tallies.
- Inter-quartile range of winning percentage is about 30% in basketball and football versus about 20% in hockey and baseball (p.7).
- The home-team-always model is the null baseline; average home win probability across sports 53-61% (p.4).
- Pooled quartic coefficients for all four sports (Tables A1-A4, p.11), which allow the NBA accuracy curve to be reproduced.

### Relevance to an Elo-based Monte Carlo championship simulator
The paper gives a 65-68% within-season Bradley-Terry accuracy for the NBA that bounds what more games of history can add, shows margin of victory adds nothing for NBA winner prediction, and provides a raw higher-seed first-round series win rate of about 73% that the series-level part of the backtest can be checked against.
