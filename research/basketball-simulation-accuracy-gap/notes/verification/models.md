## 1. Omidiran 2013

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "Penalized Regression Models for the NBA". Author: Dapo Omidiran, University of California, Berkeley (p. 1 footnote). Stamp: "arXiv:1301.3523v1 [stat.AP] 15 Jan 2013" (p. 1). No venue named; arXiv preprint only. No licence or copyright line anywhere (checked all 24 pages). File name is correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. notes/player_level_and_ml_models.md line 14: Omidiran "fits a Subspace Prior Regression on player ratings for 2010-11 and reports that, 'with a sufficiently large training set, the model outperforms' the Las Vegas lines; the abstract gives no win rate or error figure".
   - Season and method: **confirmed** (abstract p. 1; Section 5 p. 11).
   - The quoted words are **not in the paper**. The abstract (p. 1) says: "show that with a sufficiently large number of games to train on our model outperforms those lines"; the introduction (p. 2) says "given sufficient training data SPR outperforms the Las Vegas wagering lines"; the conclusion (p. 17) says "with sufficient training data, SPR seems to better predict the NBA than Vegas". **Corrected**: use one of these verbatim.
   - "the abstract gives no win rate or error figure": confirmed for the abstract, but the body does (see below), so the sentence should no longer imply the figures are unavailable.
2. notes/player_level_and_ml_models.md line 32: "Omidiran's exact accuracy/ATS record against Vegas and the number of training games needed were not in the abstract." **Corrected** — the body gives them:
   - Training on the first 820 games of 2010-11, testing on the last 410; betting rule: bet when the model's margin differs from the Vegas line by more than Δ = 3 points (p. 12). SPR: 290 bets, net +42, **57.24 percent** winning; least squares (APM) 356 bets, 51.97 percent; ridge 346 bets, 53.76 percent; dummy (every player 0, home court +3.5) 263 bets, 49.43 percent (Table 4, p. 13). Break-even stated as "roughly 52.5%" (p. 12).
   - Robustness: training on the first 410 games, testing on 820, Δ raised to 5: SPR 503 bets, 55.67 percent; LS 50.43; ridge 51.06; dummy 48.54 (Table 6, p. 14).
   - [own computation] 57.24 percent on 290 bets has binomial SE 2.9 points (95 percent interval 51.5-62.9); against the 52.5 percent break-even z = 1.63. 55.67 percent on 503 bets: SE 2.2 (51.3-60.0), z = 1.43. Neither record is significant at the 5 percent level, and both come from a single season.
3. notes/elo_and_market_benchmarks.md line 55: "A penalized-regression player-rating paper reports mean absolute error in predicting NBA margin of victory of about 10.55 for its best model vs 10.54 for a dummy baseline (not a closing-spread figure)". **Confirmed** (Table 2 p. 7: "Mean of |Ê_i|" 10.5394 dummy, 10.5540 SPR; text p. 12: "10.54 for the dummy estimator and 10.55 with SPR"). The metric is mean absolute error, as the note says. An earlier draft called it "RMSE": the paper's RMSE row is labelled "RMSE (millions)" with values 547.0447 (dummy) and 539.7495 (SPR) (Table 2 p. 7), which is not an interpretable points figure; do not quote it as an RMSE in points. The 10.55 vs 10.54 figure is MAE, not RMSE.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Straight-up winner accuracy on the 410-game holdout (p. 7 Table 2, "Fraction of games guessed wrong"): SPR 71.46 percent, ridge 67.07, least squares 66.34, dummy (home team always) 60.73. On the 820-game holdout (p. 14 Table 5): SPR 69.51, ridge 62.68, LS 59.27, dummy 59.76. These are player-level ratings trained on one-third to two-thirds of a single season, so they sit at the top of the report's 66-74 percent band but on 410-820 games.
- The dummy estimator's home-court term is 3.5 points (p. 7), and the home team won 60.73 percent of the last 410 games of 2010-11 (Table 2) — consistent with the report's claim that 3.5 points / 100 Elo is high for the modern league.
- SPR with pairwise box-score interactions (SPR2) was worse than SPR (wrong-winner fraction 29.51 vs 28.54 percent; MAE 11.81 vs 10.55), attributed to overfitting (p. 17).
- Cross-validation chose essentially the smallest lasso penalty (λ1 = 2^-10) with ridge-type shrinkage toward box-score ratings (Table 3 p. 11) — the gain comes from the box-score prior, not sparsity.
- Cervone et al. (below) cite "Omidiran, D. (2011), 'A New Look at Adjusted Plus/Minus for Basketball Analysis,' MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference" (cervone p. 31) — a 2011 Sloan player-rating paper that the Sloan inventory in `sloan_papers.md` does not list.

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** A player-rating model beat the dummy and APM on margin prediction by a hair (MAE 10.55 vs 10.54) and claims a 57 percent ATS record on 290 bets in one season; the evidence that player-level ratings beat the line is thin and not statistically significant, which supports the report's conclusion that player models do not clearly close the gap to the market.

## 2. Uhrín, Šourek, Hubáček, Železný 2021

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "Optimal sports betting strategies in practice: an experimental review". Authors: Matej Uhrín, Gustav Šourek, Ondřej Hubáček, Filip Železný, Department of Computer Science, Czech Technical University in Prague (p. 1). Stamp: "arXiv:2107.08827v1 [q-fin.PM] 15 Jul 2021". No venue in the PDF. Acknowledgment: "Czech Science Foundation grant. no 20-29260S" (p. 19). No licence or copyright line (checked all 22 pages). File name correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. report.md line 36: "re-run on about 16,000 games from 2000-2015 against Pinnacle closing lines it was **0.68 accurate against the bookmaker's 0.70** and 'in a general KL-disadvantage'". **Confirmed** (p. 14: "The dataset consists of 16000 games ranging from the year 2000 to 2015"; "The odds then come from the closing line of the Pinnacle bookmaker. Notice that in this case the model is not as accurate as the bookmaker, and is thus in a general KL-disadvantage"; Table 3 p. 14: size 16000, acc_p 0.68, acc_b 0.7, n 2, margin 0.038, odds in [1.01, 41], A_KL ≈ −0.0146).
2. notes/player_level_and_ml_models.md line 17 (and line 51): same figures and the quote "the model is not as accurate as the bookmaker, and is thus in a general KL-disadvantage". **Confirmed** verbatim (p. 14).
3. report.md line 36 also says the Hubáček CNN "profited only by deliberately decorrelating from bookmaker odds and sizing bets". The decorrelation claim is about Hubáček et al. 2019 and is **not in this paper**; this paper's own profit finding is that with the model in KL-disadvantage, plain Kelly and max-Sharpe went to ruin (100 percent ruin, Table 6 p. 17) while fractional Kelly reached median final wealth 2.4 (mean 2.7, 0 percent ruin) over 1000 reshuffled runs of the test set (Table 6 p. 17), and that "a smart strategy modification can generate profits even in the statistically disadvantageous scenarios" (p. 18). The report's attribution to arXiv 2107.08827 should be split: accuracy figures and KL quote from Uhrín; the decorrelation design from Hubáček 2019 (not verified here).
4. "KL-disadvantage" definition: A_KL is "a statistical measure of the difference of the predictive performances (cross-entropy) of the player and the bookmaker" (p. 13); growth of Kelly profit is "directly proportional to the KL advantage" (p. 13). Worth stating when the quote is used.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Pinnacle closing-line margin on NBA money lines: 0.038 (3.8 percent) over the 16,000 games (Table 3 p. 14) — a direct figure for the vig the report assumes when it calls the no-vig line the ceiling.
- Market setting: rounds of 10 parallel games (p. 14); models trained in time order so all predictions are out-of-sample (p. 15).
- Comparison across sports (Tables 2-4, pp. 14-15): horse racing model 0.512 vs public 0.503 (A_KL +0.0022); football model 0.523 vs Pinnacle opening 0.537 (A_KL −0.013). The NBA is the case with the largest model deficit to the bookmaker.
- The paper says explicitly that it "does not elaborate on the models in details beyond their predictive performances" (p. 13): the 0.68/0.70 are the only model-quality numbers, no Brier or log loss.

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** The only clean closing-line benchmark of a player-level model in the corpus: 0.68 vs 0.70 on 16,000 games, i.e. the same 2-point deficit the report measures for team Elo. It also quantifies the vig (3.8 percent) to remove when treating the line as the ceiling.

## 3. Petridis and Pelechrinis 2026

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "Lineup Regularized Adjusted Plus-Minus (L-RAPM): Basketball Lineup Ratings with Informed Priors". Authors: Christos Petridis (Temple University), Konstantinos Pelechrinis (University of Pittsburgh) (p. 1). Stamp: "arXiv:2601.15000v1 [cs.LG] 21 Jan 2026". No venue; 7 pages. No licence or copyright line. File name correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. report.md line 36: "a team uses more than 600 lineups per season, so the average lineup has about 25-30 possessions of data". **Confirmed** from the abstract (p. 1: "a National Basketball Association (NBA) team will use more than 600 lineups during a season, which translates to an average lineup having seen the court in approximately 25-30 possessions"). Caveat: the body gives different figures — "an average lineup plays approximately 17 possessions on offense and 17 on defense, with a standard deviation of 56 possessions" (p. 1, 2023-24 data) and "A typical lineup will play an average of 30 to 40 total possession for a whole season" (p. 6). The paper is internally inconsistent (25-30, 34, 30-40); the report should say "roughly 25-40 possessions" or cite the abstract figure with that caveat.
2. notes/player_level_and_ml_models.md line 15: quote "provides improved predictive power than the currently used baseline, with the gain growing as lineup samples shrink". **Corrected**: the abstract reads "L-RAPM provides improved predictive power than the currently used baseline, and this improvement increases as the sample size for the lineups gets smaller" (p. 1). The note's version is a paraphrase presented as a quote.
3. notes line 15 "No betting-line benchmark appears": **confirmed** — there is none. The baseline is raw lineup offensive/defensive rating; the metric is RMSE of points per possession in an expanding-window weekly forecast of 2023-24 (p. 5).
4. sloan_papers.md line 91 ("Lineup sparsity ... 25-30 possessions ... (2026; not Sloan)"): confirmed as above, with the same caveat.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Data: 2022-23 (player RAPM priors) and 2023-24 (evaluation), regular season and playoffs, possession level (p. 4). Player ratings are Sill-style RAPM with λ_off = 4000, λ_def = 6000 (p. 3); players new to the league get RAPM −1 (p. 5).
- Relative RMSE improvement over raw ratings for previously unseen lineups ≈ 5 percent (p. 6, Fig. 4); the gain is largest for lineups with few possessions and vanishes above about 500 possessions (p. 5).
- "even a 1.5% improvement adds up quickly over the possessions of a whole game to approximately 3.4 points ... this is higher than the home edge that betting markets incorporate in their handicap, which is about 2 points at the moment Lopez et al. (2018)" (p. 6). The "about 2 points" market home edge is a usable comparison for the report's home-court section (100 Elo ≈ 3.5 points).
- "Sill (2010) introduced the regularized version of the APM model (RAPM) that has more predictive power" (p. 2) — supports the report's "origin of RAPM" attribution.

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** Quantifies why lineup-level simulation is data-starved (tens of possessions per lineup) and shows the gain from informed priors is a few percent of per-possession RMSE, with no game-outcome or market comparison; it supports the report's "arithmetic wall" argument but does not show lineup models beating team ratings on game outcomes.

## 4. Cervone, D'Amour, Bornn, Goldsberry 2016

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "A Multiresolution Stochastic Process Model for Predicting Basketball Possession Outcomes". Authors: Daniel Cervone (NYU), Alex D'Amour (Harvard), Luke Bornn (Simon Fraser), Kirk Goldsberry (Harvard) (p. 1). Stamp: "arXiv:1408.0777v3 [stat.AP] 25 Feb 2016". The PDF does not name a journal (the JASA publication is not stated in the file). Funding and data acknowledgments on p. 1 ("STATS LLC in partnership with the NBA for providing the optical tracking data"). No licence or copyright line (checked all 31 pages). File name correct (v3 is dated 2016; the first arXiv version was 2014, which the Sloan note already records).

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. sloan_papers.md line 21: 2014 Sloan "POINTWISE" paper; "journal version 'A Multiresolution Stochastic Process Model for Predicting Basketball Possession Outcomes.' — arXiv 1408.0777. It predicts points within a possession, not game winners; I could not open the PDF to extract its evaluation numbers." **Confirmed** that it predicts expected possession value (EPV), "the expected number of points the offense will score on a particular possession conditional on that possession's evolution up to time t" (p. 3), not game winners. The PDF does not mention the Sloan "POINTWISE" title, so the link between the two is not verifiable from this file.
2. sloan_papers.md line 81 "POINTWISE/EPV evaluation metrics (2014) were not retrievable." **Corrected** — the evaluation is: 90/10 train/test split of 2013-14 tracking data; out-of-sample log-likelihood (in thousands) of the macrotransition entry/exit models for four specifications (Table 1 p. 19), e.g. shot attempt −48.9 (constant per-player hazard) → −46.4 (covariates) → −40.9 (+ spatial) → −40.7 (full hierarchical); made basket −6.6 → −6.6 → −5.6 → −5.2. "there are no existing results to benchmark the predictive performance of our estimates" (p. 18). No game-level accuracy, Brier or market comparison exists in the paper.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Scale: 461 ballhandlers in 2013-14 (p. 11); 25 Hz tracking; design matrix of 30.4 million rows (p. 19); each macrotransition type takes about 24 hours on one processor with 120 GB RAM, microtransition models 461 processors × up to 18 hours (pp. 18-19). "The computational requirements of estimating EPV curves ... likely limit EPV discussions to academic circles and professional basketball teams" (p. 25).
- Top EPV-added per game 2013-14: Durant 3.26, James 2.96 (Table 2 p. 23) — a player value scale in points per game that can be compared with the report's "3.5-5.5 points of spread for a star".
- The model excludes fouls and free throws (p. 5 footnote) and uses constant team rebound probabilities (p. 25).

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** Shows what the richest possession-level model in the literature actually evaluates (transition log-likelihoods within possessions); it never produces or tests a game forecast, so it cannot be cited as evidence either way on game-level accuracy. Its cost figures support the report's view that possession simulation is not a practical route past the market.

## 5. Franks, D'Amour, Cervone, Bornn 2016

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "Meta-Analytics: Tools for Understanding the Statistical Properties of Sports Metrics". Authors: Alexander Franks (University of Washington), Alexander D'Amour (UC Berkeley), Daniel Cervone (NYU), Luke Bornn (Simon Fraser) (p. 1 footnote). Stamp: "arXiv:1609.09830v1 [stat.AP] 30 Sep 2016"; dated "October 3, 2016" (p. 1). No venue in the PDF (the JQAS and Sloan attributions in the notes are not verifiable from this file). Funding footnote lists NSF 1461435, DARPA FA8750-14-2-0117, ARO W911NF-15-1-0172, Amazon, NSERC (p. 1). No licence or copyright line (34 pages). File name correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. sloan_papers.md line 25: "Scores NBA and NHL metrics on discrimination, stability and independence (team-strength and player-metric reliability rather than game forecasts)". Discrimination/stability/independence on NBA and NHL metrics: **confirmed** (abstract p. 1; Section 4 pp. 11-17: 70 NBA metrics and 40 NHL metrics from 2000 onward). "team-strength" is **not in the paper**: every metric evaluated is a player metric (box score, PER, BPM, WS, VORP, Corsi, etc.; glossary pp. 32-34). **Corrected**: "player-metric reliability rather than game forecasts".
2. sloan_papers.md line 94: "stability is the fraction of a metric's variance due to true skill change rather than chance". **Corrected** — the paper defines stability as "how much we expect a single player metric to vary over time after removing chance variability" (p. 7), with S_m = 1 − E_m[V_pm[X] − V_spm[X]] / (V_m[X] − E_m[V_spm[X]]) (eq. 4, p. 8); under the mixed model S_m = σ²_PM / (σ²_PM + σ²_SM + σ²_SPM) (eq. 5, p. 8), i.e. the fraction of chance-free variance that is stable between-player variance; "if a metric is constant over time, then S_m = 1" (p. 8). A metric with a lot of true skill change has *low* stability, the opposite of the note's wording. Discrimination is the "fraction ... of between-player variance in m (in season s) due to true differences in player ability" (p. 7).
3. The earlier draft's "reliability framing": the paper's framing is "reliability" in the psychometric sense (Cronbach's alpha, p. 4), with three meta-metrics, plus a proposed fourth, "relevance", which "could ... [be] a quantitative summary of the causal or predictive relationship between the metric and an outcome of interest, like wins" (p. 25). The paper states its meta-metrics "are not designed to provide any information about how relevant the metrics are for the sport" (p. 24). So it is **not** evidence about forecast accuracy, and the report does not cite it as such (correct).

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- "over 50% of the variation in three point percentage between players in a given season is due to chance" (p. 13); empirical Bayes shrinkage improves both stability and discrimination (p. 19-20).
- Redundancy: the first principal component explains 73-75 percent of the variance in {WS, VORP, PER, BPM, PTS} (p. 13 says 75 percent, Fig. 3 caption and Fig. 10 caption say 73 percent); 15 of 65-70 components explain over 75 percent of all NBA metric variance (p. 13).
- Omnibus rate metrics (BPM, WS/48, ORtg, DRtg) have stability around 0.5-0.75 and discrimination around 0.6-0.8 (Fig. 2 p. 14) — i.e. a quarter to a half of season-to-season variation in a player's omnibus rating is real change or context, which bears on how much roster information a pre-season rating can carry.

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** Indirectly relevant: it quantifies how noisy single-season player metrics are (half of 3P% variance is chance; omnibus ratings stability 0.5-0.75), which bounds how much a roster-aware adjustment built from public player metrics can add over a team rating.

## 6. Tian, Shi, Shen, Song 2023

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "A Spectral Approach for the Dynamic Bradley-Terry Model". Authors: Xin-Yu Tian and Jian Shi (Academy of Mathematics and Systems Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences; equal contribution), Xiaotong Shen (University of Minnesota; corresponding), Kai Song (AMSS) (p. 1). Stamp: "arXiv:2307.16642v2 [stat.ME] 5 Aug 2023". No venue. No licence or copyright line (29 pages). File name correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. sloan_papers.md line 69: "Dynamic Bradley-Terry (arXiv 2307.16642) performed comparably to FiveThirtyEight's public NBA Elo (Silver and Fischer-Baum 2015) and beat it in one bandwidth setting." **Confirmed** (Table 1 p. 15; text p. 14: "Somewhat surprisingly, our method achieved comparable results to Elo and outperformed Elo when h = 1 in the average accuracy"). Details to add: Kernel Rank Centrality (KRC) with Gaussian kernel; data 2009-10 to 2018-19 from nba.com, first seven seasons as base, test on 2016-17, 2017-18, 2018-19 (p. 13-14); winner predicted as "the team with a larger skill score" (p. 14), so it is a pick accuracy with no probabilities and no explicit home-court term. Results (Table 1 p. 15), total over the three test seasons: Elo 0.6355; KRC h=1 0.6382; KRC h=0.5 0.6341; KRC h=0.1 0.6301; KRC h=1.5 0.6173; WMLE h=1 0.6355; static rank centrality 0.5927; static MLE 0.5946. [own computation] The KRC(h=1) edge over Elo is 0.27 points, about 10 games in roughly 3,690. Per season Elo won 2016-17 (0.6309 vs 0.6455 — KRC ahead), 2017-18 Elo 0.6325 vs KRC 0.6325 (tie), 2018-19 Elo 0.6431 vs KRC 0.6366 (Elo ahead).
2. The abstract's and discussion's stronger wording — "Data analysis using NBA statistics indicates that our ranking method outperforms the esteemed Elo method in forecasting accuracy" (p. 16) — is **not supported by Table 1** except at one bandwidth and by 0.27 points; the notes' "comparably ... beat it in one bandwidth setting" is the accurate reading.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Elo pick accuracy of 63.6 percent over 2016-17 to 2018-19 (Table 1 p. 15) is 2-4 points below the 66-68 percent Elo band in the report. The paper does not say whether its Elo comparison included the home-court bonus or FiveThirtyEight's full probability; it describes Elo as "a feature-rich method that uses more feature data to adjust the margin of victory than KRC" (p. 14). If home court was omitted, this is a direct measure of what the home term contributes (about 2-4 points of accuracy) — worth noting as a hypothesis, not a finding.
- The model assumes game outcomes are independent Bernoulli with time-varying strengths (Assumption A, p. 4) and the authors note that "Factors such as injuries, referees, and others may impact game results, causing them to deviate from the dynamic Bradley-Terry model" (p. 15).
- Paired win-probability confidence intervals at the end of 2017-18 (Table 2 p. 16): Warriors over Rockets 0.51 (0.46, 0.57) — an example of a rating model with uncertainty quantification that puts the two best teams at a coin flip.

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** A time-varying Bradley-Terry fitted only to wins and losses lands within a third of a point of Elo on pick accuracy over three seasons; a different team-rating family does not move the needle, which supports the report's "Elo-tier" ceiling for win-loss-only ratings.

## 7. Maddox, Sides, Harvill 2022

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "Bayesian estimation of in-game home team win probability for National Basketball Association games". Authors: Jason T. Maddox (Sport Management, Syracuse University), Ryan Sides (Mathematics and Computer Science, Texas Woman's University), Jane L. Harvill (Statistical Science, Baylor University) (p. 1). Stamp: "arXiv:2207.05114v1 [stat.AP] 11 Jul 2022"; dated "July 13, 2022" (p. 1). No venue. No licence or copyright line (15 pages). File name correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. report.md line 28: "TeamRankings pre-game probabilities scored Brier 0.2167 against Elo's 0.2179 ([arXiv 2207.05114])". **Confirmed** (Table 5 p. 13: pregame model Brier TeamRankings.com 0.2167, Elo 0.2179; in-game model 0.1598 vs 0.1605). Context to add: test data are the 2018-19 and 2019-20 regular seasons (2019-20 up to the COVID stoppage; postseason excluded) (pp. 3-4, 9); the Elo is FiveThirtyEight's historical Elo (p. 12); the paper does not say how Elo ratings were converted to a probability.
2. notes/championship_forecast_evaluation.md line 108: same figures "(a 0.25 coin flip baseline)". Figures **confirmed**; the "0.25 coin flip baseline" is the note's own gloss, **not in the paper**.
3. sloan_papers.md line 68: "TeamRankings.com pregame probabilities had a lower Brier score than Elo, with a small gap." **Confirmed** (0.0012).
4. Independence assumption: the paper models the number of home wins in a (time, lead) window as "distributed as a binomial(N_{t,ℓ}, p_{t,ℓ}) random variable" (p. 4), i.e. games in a window are independent with a common win probability; the Brier score sums over every second of every game, Q = 6,590,576 observation-seconds per test season (p. 9), treating each second as an observation. The paper never discusses dependence between games or within a series. notes/player_level_and_ml_models.md line 132 attributes an independence assumption to "Deshpande and Jensen (2016)" via a different source (arXiv 2301.04001), not to this paper; this paper cites Deshpande and Jensen only for the "pseudo-wins/pseudo-losses" interpretation of the beta prior (p. 6). Nothing to correct, but if the report wants an in-game-model independence example from a read paper, Maddox p. 4 and p. 9 is it.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Home team won "approximately 59%" of the 7,376 training games, 2012-13 to 2017-18 regular seasons (p. 11) — another data point for the home-court section (the report's backtest window shows 58.3 percent).
- In-game Brier over 2018-19 + 2019-20: ESPN 0.1582, adjusted dynamic Bayes 0.1598, dynamic Bayes without pregame information 0.1697 (Table 3 p. 12); ESPN is better overall but the Bayes model wins at several fourth-quarter checkpoints (Table 4 p. 12). Adding pregame team strength to the in-game model is worth 0.010 Brier (0.1697 → 0.1598).
- Prior from a poll of 14 NBA experts including front-office staff (p. 5).
- Pregame probability enters with a weight that decays with time and score (quadratic weight function B3, p. 10); the Brier gain from the fancier weight is 0.1622 → 0.1598 (Table 2 p. 11).

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** Confirms that at the pre-game level a commercial power rating (TeamRankings) and FiveThirtyEight Elo are 0.001 Brier apart over two seasons, and that both sit around 0.217, consistent with the report's Elo-tier Brier of 0.213-0.218.

## 8. Yeh, Rice, Dubin 2020

**Bibliographic facts (PDF).** Title "Evaluating real-time probabilistic forecasts with application to National Basketball Association outcome prediction". Authors: Chi-Kuang Yeh, Gregory Rice (Statistics and Actuarial Science, University of Waterloo), Joel A. Dubin (Statistics and Actuarial Science; School of Public Health and Health Systems, Waterloo) (p. 1). Stamp: "arXiv:2010.00781v1 [stat.ME] 2 Oct 2020"; dated October 5, 2020. No venue in the PDF (the notes' "The American Statistician 76(3), 214-223 (2022)" cannot be verified from this file). No licence or copyright line (35 pages). File name correct.

**Claims attributed to this paper.**

1. sloan_papers.md line 70: ESPN in-game NBA win probabilities "are well-calibrated and beat naive baselines but show 'no significantly improved skill over simple logistic regression models based on relative team strength and score difference'; Brier loss." Substance **confirmed**; the quoted words are **not verbatim**. The abstract (p. 1) says the forecasts "are well-calibrated, and exhibit improved skill over several naïve models, but do not demonstrate significantly improved skill over simple logistic regression models based solely on a measurement of each teams' relative strength, and the evolving score difference throughout the game." Brier score is the loss used (p. 16). **Corrected**: quote the abstract text or drop the quotation marks.
2. notes/championship_forecast_evaluation.md line 32: "found them 'well calibrated' and better than naive baselines but not clearly better than a simple logistic regression on team strength". **Confirmed** in substance ("well-calibrated" appears on p. 1 and p. 4); the logistic model is on team strength *and score difference* (PgRSScD), so "on team strength" alone understates it. Minor correction.
3. Claim checked: "no significant skill over a simple logistic model (Brier)". Confirmed with the precise finding (p. 26): "In absolute terms, the estimated skill as measured by the Brier score generally favoured the simple logit model PgRSScD, with the exception of the last moments in the game. However ... the difference is apparently not statistically significant at the 5% level at any game time point ... nor is it significant when the difference is aggregated across time points." So the simple logit was *numerically better* than ESPN for most of the game.

**Figures the report does not yet use.**
- Relative team strength in the benchmark models is ESPN's own pre-game probability, RS_i = p̂^ESPN_i(0) (p. 7-8); the authors say alternatives gave similar results but none is reported, so the paper contains no independent pre-game accuracy figure.
- Historic home win rate 0.593 over NBA regular seasons 2008-2017 (p. 8), and the simulation is calibrated to "approximately 59%" (p. 21) — another home-court data point.
- Data: ESPN in-game forecasts began in 2017-18 (p. 5); 1,137 games (2017-18, training) and 1,213 games (2018-19, test), 460-480 events per game (Table 1 p. 6); overtime periods removed (p. 6).
- Methodological point relevant to the report's championship test: the skill test "might struggle to differentiate competitive models without a large sample size (N ≥ 500 in the examples considered)" (p. 24) — at N = 100-250 games the power to separate a correctly specified logit from a score-only model was 0.38-0.75 at the 5 percent level (Table 3 p. 26). This supports the report's low-power argument, at the game level.
- Calibration method: adaptive (rank-based) bins with Wilson intervals, Bonferroni-corrected across 10 bins (pp. 11-12); forecasts above 0.995 or below 0.005 handled separately — a template for the report's recommended calibration diagnostics.

**Relevance to an Elo Monte Carlo.** A proprietary, information-rich in-game model did not beat a two-covariate logistic regression on Brier at any point over 1,213 games; the lesson for a simulator is that added model richness does not reliably buy skill, and that detecting a small skill difference needs several hundred games even at the game level.
