# Team-level rating systems vs the betting market: measured accuracy and the predictability ceiling for NBA games

Method note: the source pages could not be opened in the research session, so every finding below comes from search-result snippets and summaries rather than from reading the full source pages. Where a snippet cut off before a number, that is recorded as a gap rather than guessed. Items marked **[computed]** are own arithmetic from cited inputs. Update, 2026-10-23: the figures attributed to the 26 papers now in `research/papers/` have been checked against the full PDFs; the corrected passages below carry page numbers, and the per-paper verdicts are in `notes/verification/`. ## Key question 1: What accuracy / log loss / Brier do FiveThirtyEight's NBA Elo (and later CARMELO and RAPTOR) achieve, and how did they compare with the Vegas line?

### Takeaway
Across every independent evaluation found, FiveThirtyEight-style NBA Elo picks the straight-up winner about 66–68% of the time with a Brier score around 0.21 and log loss around 0.61–0.62, and in every head-to-head comparison the closing betting line is better by roughly 2 percentage points of accuracy and about 0.008–0.009 Brier; FiveThirtyEight never published a game-level accuracy or Brier table of its own that this research could locate, only a calibration-focused "checking our work" tracker.

### Cited Findings
- FiveThirtyEight's own forecast-evaluation project judges forecasts "mainly by calibration", reports Brier skill scores against a naive baseline, and its headline is that events forecast at 70% occurred 71% of the time (across all sports and politics combined); no NBA-specific game accuracy or Brier number was retrievable from the snippets — [FiveThirtyEight "Checking our work"](https://projects.fivethirtyeight.com/checking-our-work); [FiveThirtyEight "When we say 70 percent, it really means 70 percent"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/when-we-say-70-percent-it-really-means-70-percent)
- Albert Kuo's critique of that tracker: FiveThirtyEight's "primary metric they use to evaluate their model is calibration", and the models are "generally well-calibrated"; Kuo argues calibration alone is insufficient because an always-50% model is perfectly calibrated — [Albert Kuo, "How Good is FiveThirtyEight's NBA Prediction Model?" (2022)](https://blog.albertkuo.me/post/2022-01-21-how-good-is-fivethirtyeight-s-nba-prediction-model/)
- Independent replication of 538's NBA Elo (Nic Dobson): re-implementation "matched the original results"; Elo variants picked winners correctly about 67–68% of the time; Elo was "really robust to configuration" (good results with K of 20, 35, 50; home court anywhere from 50 to 150 Elo) — [nicidob, "FiveThirtyEight's Elo Ratings and Logistic Regression"](https://nicidob.github.io/nba_elo/)
- A GitHub Elo replication on 2010–2015 data (7,641 games) reports 67.2% accuracy for its from-scratch Elo versus 67.3% for FiveThirtyEight's published forecast — [gakoduah/NBA-Game-Prediction-Elo](https://github.com/gakoduah/NBA-Game-Prediction-Elo)
- Ergo Sum replication of Nate Silver's NBA Elo (2017): ~68% straight-up winner accuracy; against-the-spread performance "not statistically good enough to beat Vegas spread lines" — [Ergo Sum, "Replicating Nate Silver's NBA Elo Algorithm"](https://www.ergosum.co/nate-silvers-nba-elo-algorithm/)
- 2019–20 season, 503 games through 31 Dec 2019: 538 Elo picked 340/503 winners (67.6%) vs RAPTOR 331/503 (65.8%); two-proportion z-test not significant; Elo slightly overconfident (expected 344.9 correct vs 340 actual) — [Andrew Kyne, "How accurate are FiveThirtyEight's NBA game forecasts?"](https://andrewkyne.wordpress.com/2020/01/04/fivethirtyeight-nba-forecast-accuracy-raptor/)
- 2019–20 season, 679 games (late Oct 2019 – late Jan 2020): RAPTOR picked the winner 66.4%, Elo 67.0%; where they disagreed (108 games) Elo was right 51.9%; the "tendency for FiveThirtyEight to overweight home court advantage is clear" in residuals — [vandomed, "FiveThirtyEight's NBA Predictions: RAPTOR vs. ELO"](https://vandomed.medium.com/fivethirtyeights-nba-predictions-raptor-vs-elo-254de5278645)
- Same author, same season: simple team metrics (win percentage, points-per-game differential) outperformed the 538 Elo forecast once a burn-in of at least 7 days was used — [vandomed, "FiveThirtyEight's NBA Predictions are Underperforming Simple Team Metrics"](https://vandomed.medium.com/fivethirtyeights-nba-predictions-are-underperforming-simple-team-metrics-6baa79a39fd6)
- 2017–18 season calibration check of 538's NBA forecasts: games given a 70–74% chance were won only ~61% of the time, while the 75–79% bin hit 77.2% — [Jake Galant, "How did FiveThirtyEight NBA Predictions do in the 2017–2018 Season?"](https://medium.com/@jakegalant/how-did-fivethirtyeight-nba-predictions-do-in-the-2017-2018-season-f60fecf576ba)
- RAPTOR calibration critique: RAPTOR "predicted the home team will win in 70% of games, but home teams only win about 61% of the time" (season not given in snippet) — [BinomialBasketball, "FiveThirtyEight's RAPTOR Model is not Calibrated"](https://medium.com/@BinomialBasketball/fivethirtyeights-raptor-model-is-not-calibrated-6b247ae0d1ee)
- CARM-Elo vs Vegas spreads, 2016–17 regular season (1,230 games): 538's spread differed from Vegas in 1,136 games; betting the 538 side went 559–560–17, "no better than a coin flip" — [Stanford Sports Analytics, "Comparing FiveThirtyEight.com's CARM-Elo Predictions to Las Vegas Point Spreads"](https://stanfordsportsanalytics.wordpress.com/2017/06/18/in-search-of-a-winning-strategy-comparing-fivethirtyeight-coms-carm-elo-predictions-to-las-vegas-point-spreads/)
- RAPTOR-derived point spreads would not beat sportsbook lines; author suggests a blend of RAPTOR and sportsbook lines "might actually be the optimal solution" (MAE figures not in snippet) — [Walker Harrison, "Evaluating the NBA point spreads produced by FiveThirtyEight's RAPTOR metric" (2022)](https://www.walker-harrison.com/posts/2022-05-27-evaluating-the-point-spreads-produced-by-538-s-raptor-metric/)
- 538 vs bookmakers on NBA playoff-berth probabilities (2021 post): Brier 0.143 (538) vs 0.139 (bookmakers), difference 0.004, p = 0.881, "statistically indistinguishable" — [R-bloggers, "Predicting NBA Playoff Berths: FiveThirtyEight vs Betting Markets"](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2021/12/predicting-nba-playoff-berths-fivethirtyeight-vs-betting-markets/)
- 2021–22 preseason win totals: where 538 and Vegas differed by at least 2 games, 538 was closer on 8 (near coin flip), but Vegas's mean absolute error was almost a full game lower overall — [Save the Lottery, "Mostly Harmless 21/22 NBA Prognostications"](https://savethelottery.substack.com/p/mostly-harmless-2122-nba-prognostications)
- Walk-forward Elo vs closing market, 2012-13 to 2022-23 out-of-sample (13,486 games; data 2007-08 to Jan 2023, 19,822 games): Elo Brier 0.2146 / log loss 0.6177; logistic model Brier 0.2141; no-vig closing moneyline Brier 0.2055 / log loss 0.5965 / 67.8% accuracy; market beat the model in every test season; encompassing regression: market coefficient 0.97 (SE 0.045), model coefficient −0.001 (SE 0.047, p = 0.98), i.e. the model adds no information beyond the closing price — [DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets)
- Another independent Elo + schedule-features model: log loss 0.6131, Brier 0.2126, 66.1% accuracy vs Vegas closing line log loss 0.5918, Brier 0.2035, 68.1%; author concludes "our best estimate ties the closing line, and we have no betting edge" (~50% against the spread); seasons not stated in snippet — [nhorto/NBA](https://github.com/nhorto/NBA)
- Other Elo-type NBA figures: 68.2% holdout accuracy on 2,462 games (Elo + home court + rest) — [raghavchhabra123/nba-matchup-predictor](https://github.com/raghavchhabra123/nba-matchup-predictor); Bryant University thesis: logistic regression / random forest with Elo as a feature, 66.95–67.15% — [Bryant University honors thesis, "Predicting the Outcome of NBA Games"](https://digitalcommons.bryant.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1000&context=honors_data_science); an Elo-only model at 65.3% — [luke-lite/NBA-Prediction-Modeling](https://github.com/luke-lite/NBA-Prediction-Modeling); a Wolfram Community ML study found the Elo + location classifier was the most accurate of the options tried — [Wolfram Community, "Using Machine Learning to Predict NBA Games"](https://community.wolfram.com/groups/-/m/t/1730466)
- A university rating-system health page reports Brier 0.201 and log loss 0.589 across 1,132 games but flags these as optimistically biased because season-end ratings were applied retroactively; it states "69–72% is typical for mature rating systems" — [USU NBA Model Health](https://macfax.usu.edu/nba/model-health)
- Harvard Sports Analysis (2019) tuned 538's Elo using log loss as the objective, motivated by how early-season vs late-season games should be weighted; the snippet cut off before the baseline and improved numbers — [HSAC, "A Simple Improvement to FiveThirtyEight's NBA Elo Model"](https://harvardsportsanalysis.org/2019/01/a-simple-improvement-to-fivethirtyeights-nba-elo-model/)

### Inferences
- The consistent independent picture for a 538-style team Elo on regular-season NBA games is: accuracy 66–68%, Brier 0.21–0.215, log loss 0.61–0.62. A reasonable benchmark table for an Elo Monte Carlo model: "Elo-tier" = 67% / 0.213 / 0.615; "market-tier" = 68% / 0.204–0.206 / 0.592–0.597.
- The gap between Elo and the closing market is small in accuracy (~2 points) but systematic: it appears in every season tested in the 11-season sample, and the encompassing test shows the market already contains all the Elo information. For an Elo Monte Carlo model, this means a season-simulation that only consumes Elo cannot be expected to beat market-derived inputs; the achievable improvement from better team-strength estimation alone is on the order of 0.01 Brier.
- RAPTOR (player-based) did not improve on team Elo at the game level in the only season both were compared (2019–20); any accuracy gains from player-level modeling appear to be concentrated in injury/rest situations rather than in average game accuracy.
- FiveThirtyEight's published self-evaluation is calibration-oriented; the independent evidence suggests its NBA probabilities were reasonably but not perfectly calibrated (overconfidence in the 70–74% bin in 2017–18, over-weighting of home court in 2019–20).

### Gaps
- No FiveThirtyEight-published game-level accuracy, Brier or log-loss figure for NBA Elo, CARMELO or RAPTOR was retrievable; the "checking our work" NBA Brier skill score could not be read because fivethirtyeight.com was unreachable.
- The Harvard Sports Analysis post's baseline log loss and the size of its improvement were not retrievable.
- Walker Harrison's RAPTOR-vs-Vegas spread MAE figures were not retrievable.
- Seasons covered by the nhorto/NBA evaluation are unknown.

## Key question 2: Accuracy of the betting market (closing moneyline and spread) on NBA games, and the typical RMSE of the closing spread against the final margin

### Takeaway
The no-vig closing moneyline picks about 68% of NBA winners with a Brier score of roughly 0.204–0.206 and log loss near 0.59–0.60 over 2012–2023, is calibrated to within about 5 percentage points across probability bins, and the standard deviation of the final margin around the closing spread is about 12 points (so spread RMSE ≈ 12 and MAE ≈ 9–10 points), though the 12-point figure is an analytic estimate rather than a published fitted value.

### Cited Findings
- Closing market, vig removed, 2012-13 to 2022-23 (13,486 games): Brier 0.2055, log loss 0.5965, accuracy 67.8% — [DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets)
- Vegas closing line (seasons unstated): log loss 0.5918, Brier 0.2035, accuracy 68.1% — [nhorto/NBA](https://github.com/nhorto/NBA)
- Pinnacle closing moneylines, 2016–17 to 2019–20: actual and implied win probabilities "don't differ by more than 5%" in any bin; huge favorites slightly overrated, huge underdogs slightly underrated — [Towards Data Science, "Why you are betting on the wrong NBA teams"](https://towardsdatascience.com/why-you-are-betting-on-the-wrong-nba-teams-39e2bf98588/)
- A Princeton thesis compared model probabilities with sportsbook-implied probabilities; its models reached an average accuracy of 70% (this is model, not market, accuracy; sample not in snippet) — [Princeton thesis](https://theses-dissertations.princeton.edu/entities/publication/eb6e97e3-2518-426a-b4b5-c7953d928c73)
- Standard deviation of NBA margin of victory relative to the point spread is "near 12 points"; derived from ~86 FGA per team at ~50% eFG%, assuming independence of the two teams' shooting, which the author calls oversimplified — [Wayne Winston, "Why is Standard Deviation of NBA scores about point spread 12 points" (2018)](https://waynewinston.com/wordpress/p_2333/)
- An NBA prediction model documents using a normal margin distribution with standard deviation "approximately thirteen" as of March 2020 — [Spencer-Weston/NBApredict](https://github.com/Spencer-Weston/NBApredict)
- A penalized-regression player-rating paper reports mean absolute error in predicting NBA margin of victory of 10.55 points for its best model (SPR) against 10.54 for a dummy estimator that rates every player zero and gives the home team 3.5 points, on the last 410 games of 2010-11 (Table 2, p. 7); this is MAE, not RMSE, and not a closing-spread figure — [arXiv 1301.3523, "Penalized Regression Models for the NBA"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3523)
- An unrelated NBA modeling project cites Vegas spreads as having mean absolute error "around 8" (no sample given) — [Levine, "Beating Vegas Thesis", ch. 3](https://dlevine820.github.io/Beating-Vegas-Thesis/3-model.html)
- For comparison, NFL closing-line residuals have mean −0.009 and SD 13.588 — [arXiv 1211.4000, "The Performance of Betting Lines for Predicting the Outcome of NFL Games"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1211.4000); college basketball spread residuals have SD about 10.3 — [The Only Colors, "The Variance of College Basketball"](https://www.theonlycolors.com/2020/4/27/21226073/the-variance-of-college-basketball-how-big-is-it-and-where-does-it-come-from)
- The ESPN "Chalk" piece on playoff spread records of champions since 1985 exists but its numbers were not in the snippet — [ESPN Chalk](https://global.espn.com/chalk/story/_/id/19618879/nba-ranking-best-playoff-spread-records-nba-championship-teams-1985)

### Inferences
- **[computed]** If the closing-spread residual is roughly normal with SD 12, the RMSE is 12 and the MAE is 12·√(2/π) ≈ 9.6 points; SD 11 gives MAE ≈ 8.8, SD 13 gives MAE ≈ 10.4. The "MAE around 8" claim in the Levine thesis would imply SD ≈ 10, which is below the Winston/NBApredict range; I treat 11–13 points RMSE and 9–10 points MAE as the defensible benchmark range. Nothing found contradicts the 11–13 point range in the task prompt, but no source reports a directly fitted season-by-season NBA closing-spread RMSE.
- The market's game-level numbers (≈68%, Brier ≈0.205, log loss ≈0.595) are the practical ceiling for any public model: two independent long-sample comparisons agree to within 0.3 points of accuracy and 0.002 Brier.
- **[computed]** Reference points for interpreting Brier/log loss: always 50% gives Brier 0.25 and log loss ln 2 = 0.693; predicting the home team at a flat 58% gives expected Brier 0.58·0.42 = 0.244 and log loss 0.680 (assuming the true home win rate is 58%). The market's 0.205 is thus about 18% below the coin-flip Brier, and Elo's 0.213–0.215 recovers roughly 80% of the market's improvement over the coin flip.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study reporting season-by-season NBA closing-moneyline favorite win rate was found; both long-sample market figures come from personal GitHub projects.
- No published, fitted RMSE/MAE of the NBA closing spread versus the final margin by season was found; the 12-point SD is an analytic estimate.

## Key question 3: Studies estimating the upper bound of NBA outcome predictability

### Takeaway
Multiple independent approaches converge on the conclusion that even a forecaster with perfect knowledge of team strength would call only about 68–75% of NBA games correctly: the betting market (the best available proxy for true strength) sits at ~68%, luck-decomposition models estimate the underdog wins ~36% of NBA games, Bayesian state-space work finds the better of two randomly drawn teams has a median neutral-site win probability of ~67%, and machine-learning work on college basketball reports a "glass ceiling" of 74–75% that extensive tuning does not break.

### Cited Findings
- Aoki, Assunção and Vaz de Melo (KDD 2017), 198 leagues, 1,503 seasons, 4 sports: for the NBA the estimated probability that the underdog wins is ≈0.36, stable across seasons, interpreted by the authors as the luck component; the underdog wins 0.27 away and 0.45 at home, a swing of 0.18 (the abstract's 0.09 is measured from the unconditional 0.36); the skill coefficient φ for the NBA is above 0.95; the introduction states, citing Martin et al. 2016, that "there are no algorithms capable of producing accurate predictions and there is some evidence they will never be found"; the paper's own conclusion is that luck decides "about 35% of the times in NBA" — [arXiv 1706.02447, "Luck is Hard to Beat: The Difficulty of Sports Prediction"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02447); summary at [the morning paper](https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/09/05/luck-is-hard-to-beat-the-difficulty-of-sports-prediction/)
- Same paper: up to 62% of NBA teams would need removing before the league's final ranking became random (i.e., skill dispersion is wide) — [arXiv 1706.02447](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02447)
- Lopez, Matthews and Baumer (Annals of Applied Statistics 2018), Bayesian state-space models fit to betting-market probabilities for NFL, NHL, NBA, MLB over about a decade: the NBA shows "both the largest dispersion in talent and the largest home advantage"; the median probability that the better of two randomly drawn teams wins a neutral-site game is highest in the NBA at about 67% (NFL ~64%, NHL ~57%, MLB ~56%); about 27% of NBA neutral-site matchups give the best team odds above 3:1; when the best NBA team plays at home it is always favored at ≥60%, with the middle half of games between 68% and 84%; a typical NBA home team has a 62.0% chance against an equal opponent (range 60% Brooklyn to 66.1% Denver), vs 58.9% NFL, 55.5% NHL, 54% MLB — [arXiv 1701.05976, "How often does the best team win?"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05976); [Loyola eCommons copy](https://ecommons.luc.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1025&context=math_facpubs)
- Secondary summary of the same paper (not in the paper itself): in a best-of-seven NBA series the better team wins about 80% of the time. The paper reports instead a postseason parity pseudo-R-squared of 0.43 for the NBA and 0.51 for the NFL against 0.88 MLB and 0.85 NHL for simulated 16-team best-of-seven tournaments, that MLB would need roughly 75-game series to match the NBA, and that the NBA would need an 8-team single-game tournament to match the others (pp. 27-29) — [Complexity Digest summary](https://comdig.cssociety.org/2018/01/31/how-often-does-the-best-team-win-a-unified-approach-to-understanding-randomness-in-north-american-sport/)
- The paper notes that its cumulative fits, which use games before and after week k, are "ideal for measuring league parity looking backwards" but "less appropriate to make future game predictions", and uses sequential fits for prediction (p. 11); on those sequential fits the NBA AUC is 0.756 and Brier 0.194, identical to the closing market's 0.756 and 0.194 for the 2015-16 season (Table 5, p. 24) — [arXiv 1701.05976](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05976)
- Zimmermann, Moorthy and Shi (2013): "there seems to be an upper limit to predictive accuracy for match outcomes, at around 74%–75%" for NCAAB; multi-layer perceptrons performed best; the limit is attributed either to missing attributes (experience, synergies) or to "a large amount of chance in outcomes" — [arXiv 1310.3607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.3607)
- Zimmermann (2016, Statistical Analysis and Data Mining), NBA vs NCAAB: an earlier NBA study's reported 74.33% accuracy is "surprising" in light of his results and relied on extensive neural-network tuning; the NBA classifier needed about half a season to stabilize; a 2024 review cites this paper as finding Naive Bayes best for NBA, particularly for playoff series — [Zimmermann 2016 PDF](https://zimmermanna.users.greyc.fr/papers/journals/sadm2016.pdf); [arXiv 2410.21484 review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21484)
- Zimmermann (2015) "Exploring chance in NCAA basketball": simulation-based ceiling estimates that assume simple noise models are too optimistic; season-specific limits derived from team-profile clustering and schedule give "higher assessments of chance and tighter limits on predictive accuracy" — [arXiv 1508.04688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.04688); later extended to both college and professional basketball in [Springer chapter "Chance and the Predictive Limit in Basketball (Both College and Professional)"](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-45275-8_40)
- A Euroleague study cites the basketball "glass ceiling" of about 74% that "cannot be exceeded by ML or statistical techniques" — [arXiv 2002.08465](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.08465)
- A student NBA prediction project reports that the best NBA models "achieved an upper bound between 66–72% accuracy" and a regular-season upset rate of 32.1% (seasons not in snippet; not peer reviewed) — [UNR ScholarWolf, "NBA Machine Learning for Game Outcome Prediction"](https://scholarwolf.unr.edu/items/1ef8e573-20f9-4811-8c84-5f92a513526b)
- Basketball's long sequence of scoring events makes it harder for the weaker team to win by luck than in low-scoring sports — [arXiv 1501.07179, "Who's good this year? Comparing the Information Content of Games in the Four Major US Sports"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.07179)
- Encompassing test on 13,486 games: the Elo model's coefficient is ≈0 once the closing price is included (market coefficient 0.97), i.e. the market probability is empirically the sufficient statistic — [DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets)
- Historical frequency with which the best regular-season team wins the title: ~45% (35 of 78) at the start of the playoffs, 58% reach the Finals, 77.8% win once there — [Charted Interests, "How often does the best NBA team win the championship?" (2018)](https://chartedinterests.wordpress.com/2018/04/12/how-often-does-the-best-nba-team-win-the-championship/); an older Basketball-Reference simulation put the best team's title probability at about 48% (vs MLB 29%, NFL 24%, NCAAB 34%) — [Basketball-Reference blog](https://www.basketball-reference.com/blog/indexaead.html?p=3555)
- FiveThirtyEight's older Elo model implied a 52-win team wins the title ~2% of the time and a 64-win team ~50% — [FiveThirtyEight, "Every NBA team's chance of winning a title by 2019"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/every-nba-teams-chance-of-winning-a-title-by-2019)

### Inferences
- The reasoning behind the "~25–30% of games are unpredictable" claim: if the market's probabilities are well calibrated (within ~5 points per bin per the Pinnacle study) and contain all available information (encompassing coefficient 0.97, model coefficient ≈0), then the market's 67.8–68.1% accuracy is itself an estimate of the accuracy of a forecaster who knows the true pre-game probabilities. **[computed]** Perfect-knowledge accuracy equals E[max(p, 1−p)] over the distribution of true probabilities; the market's observed 68% is an unbiased estimate of that quantity if the market is calibrated, so roughly 32% of regular-season games are coin-flip-type losses even for an oracle on team strength. Aoki et al.'s 0.36 underdog win rate gives the same ~64–68% figure from a completely different (non-market) model.
- The 74–75% "glass ceiling" from the ML literature is higher than the market figure. Two reasons appear in the sources: (a) those figures are for NCAAB (Zimmermann 2015 gives season-specific NCAA limits of 71-76% and treats the NBA as future work); the paper attributes the ceiling to chance and missing attributes, not to talent dispersion, and (b) the one NBA result at 74.33% was heavily tuned and Zimmermann himself called it surprising. For NBA benchmarking, 68–70% should be treated as the realistic ceiling and 74–75% as an optimistic one.
- Series-level and season-level predictability is much higher than game-level: the home-court team won 75.7% of 305 best-of-seven series in 1947-2005 (Rump 2006, own sum of Table 4), higher seeds won 72.9% of first-round NBA series in 2010-11 to 2015-16 (Wolfson et al.), the better-record team won 73.4% of 68 Finals (Chance 2020), and the best regular-season team wins the title ~45–50% of the time; the ~80% best-of-seven figure comes from a secondary summary of Lopez et al. and is not in the paper. An Elo Monte Carlo model's series and title probabilities should therefore be judged against those base rates, not against game accuracy.

### Gaps
- No source gives a single peer-reviewed "theoretical maximum accuracy for NBA games" number for the modern NBA; the figures above are inferred from market accuracy, luck decomposition, and NCAAB-oriented ML ceilings.
- The exact NBA accuracy tables of Zimmermann (2016) and the 2024 "Chance and the Predictive Limit" chapter were not retrievable; the 2015 NCAA paper (now in research/papers/) gives limits of 0.7132-0.7467 (unconstrained clustering) or 0.7329-0.7648 (optimised) for 2008-2013 against KenPom's 0.7035-0.7307.

## Key question 4: How FiveThirtyEight's Elo parameters (K=20, home court 100, 25% regression to 1505, MOV multiplier) relate to accuracy, and what tuning showed

### Takeaway
FiveThirtyEight's published NBA Elo uses K = 20, a fixed 100-Elo (≈3.5 point) home-court bonus, 75% carry-over with reversion toward 1505, and a margin-of-victory multiplier of (MOV+3)^0.8 / (7.5 + 0.006·EloDiff); FiveThirtyEight stated it tested a dynamic home-court term and rejected it as making almost no difference, and independent tuning found Elo accuracy to be flat across wide ranges of K and home court, while several independent analyses find 100 Elo over-states home court for the modern NBA (≈64% implied vs ≈58–61% observed).

### Cited Findings
- FiveThirtyEight NBA Elo: K-factor 20, which "implies that you ought to give relatively high weight to an NBA team's recent performance"; average rating 1500 with more than 90% of teams between 1300 and 1700; teams keep three-quarters of their rating from season to season (vs two-thirds in the NFL); reversion target is 1505 "to offset recent expansion teams"; new franchises start at 1300; upset wins and larger margins earn more points — [FiveThirtyEight, "How We Calculate NBA Elo Ratings"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-we-calculate-nba-elo-ratings/)
- FiveThirtyEight set home-court advantage "equivalent to 100 Elo rating points", about 3.5 NBA points, and rejected a dynamic version "because it made almost no difference to the overall ratings" — [FiveThirtyEight, "How We Calculate NBA Elo Ratings"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-we-calculate-nba-elo-ratings)
- FiveThirtyEight's WNBA README: WNBA home court is 80 Elo "(NBA = 100)" — [fivethirtyeight/WNBA-stats README](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/WNBA-stats/blob/master/README.md)
- Margin-of-victory multiplier as replicated: G = (MOV + 3)^0.8 / (7.5 + 0.006·(winnerElo − loserElo + home adjustment)), with K = 20·G; replicators state the constants match FiveThirtyEight's — [Ergo Sum](https://www.ergosum.co/nate-silvers-nba-elo-algorithm/); [luke-lite, "How to Build an NBA Elo Rating System"](https://luke-lite.github.io/blog/how-to-build-an-NBA-Elo-rating-system/); [ddm7018/Elo](https://github.com/ddm7018/Elo)
- Basic Elo "only knows who won each game, the margin of victory and where the game was played", which is why injuries and rest were hard for it to capture and why 538 moved to CARMELO/RAPTOR blends — [FiveThirtyEight, "How our NBA predictions work"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-our-nba-predictions-work/)
- Independent tuning: "Elo is really robust to configuration", good configurations with K of 20, 35, 50 and home court anywhere from 50 to 150; the +100 home bonus is argued to be too large because the home team wins ~60% of the time, and +100 implies ~64% ("should probably be +70") — [nicidob, "FiveThirtyEight's Elo Ratings and Logistic Regression"](https://nicidob.github.io/nba_elo/)
- A college-basketball Elo that followed the 538 NBA design found optimal values K = 47, home court +82 Elo and season carry-over 0.64; it notes a Bayesian-optimization study on NBA data fit a different home-advantage term once a K-decay was added — [AnthonyErr/college-basketball-elo](https://github.com/AnthonyErr/college-basketball-elo)
- Harvard Sports Analysis used log loss as its tuning objective, arguing it rewards calibrated confidence rather than raw accuracy, and investigated weighting early-season vs late-season games — [HSAC, "A Simple Improvement to FiveThirtyEight's NBA Elo Model"](https://harvardsportsanalysis.org/2019/01/a-simple-improvement-to-fivethirtyeights-nba-elo-model/)
- Residual analysis of 2019–20 538 forecasts (679 games): "tendency for FiveThirtyEight to overweight home court advantage is clear" — [vandomed](https://vandomed.medium.com/fivethirtyeights-nba-predictions-raptor-vs-elo-254de5278645)
- NBA home win rate context: 68.5% in 1976–77, 62.8% in 2002–03, 53.7% in 2014–15 (then the modern low) — [ESPN, "Home court advantage decline"](https://africa.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/12241619/home-court-advantage-decline); about 59% since 2010 — [inpredictable (2016)](https://www.inpredictable.com/2016/01/home-court-advantage-free-throw-shooting.html?m=1); in the .540s in four of five recent seasons vs .654+ in 1980s seasons — [Marc Stein](https://marcstein.substack.com/p/what-has-happened-to-homecourt-advantage); ~54% in 2020–21 and near 54% since except 58% in 2022–23 — [Boston 25 News](https://www.boston25news.com/news/national/fact-or-fiction-is/NVE32FOQOEVRYBXQSAJNA6OTJA/)

### Inferences
- **[computed]** A 100-Elo edge implies a win probability of 1/(1+10^(−100/400)) = 64.0%; a 70-Elo edge implies 59.9%. Given observed home win rates of ~54–58% in the 2020s, the fixed 100-Elo home court is roughly 1.5–2.5 Elo-implied percentage points too generous for recent seasons, consistent with the over-weighting found in the 2019–20 residual analysis and the RAPTOR calibration critique. A season-dependent home-court parameter (or ~60–75 Elo for post-2015 seasons) is the lowest-risk tuning change.
- **[computed]** 100 Elo ≈ 3.5 points implies ≈28.6 Elo per point of spread (100/3.5).
- The flatness of accuracy across K and home-court ranges reported by Dobson explains why FiveThirtyEight found a dynamic home-court term unnecessary: for accuracy and log loss at the ~0.001–0.005 level, parameter choice barely matters; the Elo-vs-market gap (~0.009 Brier) is larger than anything parameter tuning recovers.

### Gaps
- FiveThirtyEight's own tuning results for K (what accuracy or log loss each K produced) were not retrievable; only the chosen values and the dynamic-home-court rejection are documented in snippets.

## Key question 5: Accuracy of simple baselines (home team always wins, better record wins, better seed in playoffs)

### Takeaway
"Home team wins" is correct ~54–58% of the time in the modern NBA (around 60% over 2000–2015 and 63–68% in earlier decades); "better pre-game favorite wins" (the market favorite) is correct ~64–68%; in the playoffs the higher seed won 72.9% of first-round series in 2010-11 to 2015-16 (Wolfson et al.); the ~80% best-of-seven figure comes only from a secondary summary and the No. 1 seed wins the title about two-thirds of the time.

### Cited Findings
- Home team win rate: 68.5% in 1976–77, 62.8% in 2002–03; home playoff teams won 74.4% in 2007–08; 53.7% in 2014–15, "the lowest such rate in modern NBA history" at that time — [ESPN](https://africa.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/12241619/home-court-advantage-decline)
- Home team won 57.5% overall in 2014–15 after a 62.1% rebound in the second half; about 59% since 2010 — [inpredictable](https://www.inpredictable.com/2016/01/home-court-advantage-free-throw-shooting.html?m=1)
- Home composite win percentage in the .540s in four of five recent seasons (Basketball Reference data) — [Marc Stein](https://marcstein.substack.com/p/what-has-happened-to-homecourt-advantage); 54% in 2020–21 (mostly empty arenas) and near 54% since except 58% in 2022–23 — [Boston 25 News](https://www.boston25news.com/news/national/fact-or-fiction-is/NVE32FOQOEVRYBXQSAJNA6OTJA/); home win rate about 60% over 2000–13, then about 54% in four of the next five years — [Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/fact-or-fiction-is-home-court-advantage-in-the-nbas-regular-season-dead-194958543.html)
- A typical NBA home team beats an equal opponent 62.0% of the time (fit to betting-market probabilities over ~a decade ending mid-2010s) — [arXiv 1701.05976](https://ecommons.luc.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1025&context=math_facpubs)
- Favorite (model-defined) wins ≈64% of NBA games, underdog ≈36% — [arXiv 1706.02447](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02447); closing-market favorite wins 67.8% (2012–2023) — [DuyThucCao](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets) and 68.1% (seasons unstated) — [nhorto/NBA](https://github.com/nhorto/NBA)
- Regular-season upset rate 32.1% (definition and seasons not in snippet) — [UNR ScholarWolf](https://scholarwolf.unr.edu/items/1ef8e573-20f9-4811-8c84-5f92a513526b)
- Better team wins a best-of-seven NBA series about 80% of the time according to a [Complexity Digest summary of Lopez et al.](https://comdig.cssociety.org/2018/01/31/how-often-does-the-best-team-win-a-unified-approach-to-understanding-randomness-in-north-american-sport/); the figure does not appear in the paper, which reports parity metrics only
- No. 1 seed has won the title 67.1% of the time and seeds 1–3 account for 97.5% of champions — [Sports-King](https://www.sports-king.com/nba-champions-by-playoff-seed.php); a second tally puts the No. 1 seed's share at 66.3% — [Land of Basketball](https://www.landofbasketball.com/championships/champions_by_seed.htm); No. 1 seeds are 52–38 (57.8%) in the Finals and 25–11 against non-No. 1 opponents; a No. 1 seed won 12 of the last 13 titles (as of the source's date) — [Wikipedia, NBA playoffs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NBA_playoffs)
- Best regular-season team won the title 35 of 78 times (~45%), reached the Finals 45 of 78 (58%), and won 35 of 45 Finals (77.8%) — [Charted Interests](https://chartedinterests.wordpress.com/2018/04/12/how-often-does-the-best-nba-team-win-the-championship/)
- Higher seeds sweep 22.34% of first-round series and 8.86% of Finals (1947 onward) — [nbasweep.com](https://nbasweep.com/nba-statistics/)
- The team that makes more three-pointers wins about 67% of games (in-game, not pre-game, information) — [Boston 25 News](https://www.boston25news.com/news/national/fact-or-fiction-is/NVE32FOQOEVRYBXQSAJNA6OTJA/)

### Inferences
- **[computed]** Baseline metric table for the report (assuming the stated hit rates are the true rates and a constant probability is forecast): coin flip = 50% / Brier 0.250 / log loss 0.693; home team at 58% = 58% / 0.244 / 0.680; closing favorite = 68% / ~0.205 / ~0.595 (measured, not computed). Elo at 67% / 0.213 / 0.615 sits much closer to the market than to the naive baselines.
- Because the home win rate has fallen from ~60% (2000–2015) to ~54–58% (2020s), any Elo model that fixes home court at 100 Elo (64% implied) will be systematically over-confident on home teams in recent seasons; the "home team wins" baseline must be stated with its season range.

### Gaps
- No source was found giving "team with the better record wins" accuracy for regular-season games directly; the market-favorite and model-favorite rates (64–68%) are the closest proxies.
- No single source tallies the historical rate at which the higher seed wins a playoff series across all rounds; Wolfson et al. count lower seeds winning 27.1% of first-round NBA series over 2010-11 to 2015-16, and the series-level 80% figure comes from a secondary summary and is not in Lopez et al.
