# Evaluation of NBA championship and playoff-series forecasts: published scores, baselines and the power of a 21-season comparison

Research conditions. The papers and pages could not be opened in the research session, so every web finding below comes from search-result snippets rather than full pages; where a snippet was ambiguous I say so. The one primary dataset I could obtain was FiveThirtyEight's public `checking-our-work-data` repository (cloned read-only from GitHub), and all rows marked **[own computation]** are computed from it, or from a small simulation, with scripts in `research/basketball-simulation-accuracy-gap/scripts/` (`eval538.py`, `eval538b.py`, `power.py`). Update, 2026-10-23: the figures attributed to the 26 papers now in `research/papers/` have been checked against the full PDFs; the corrected passages below carry page numbers, and the per-paper verdicts are in `notes/verification/`. Figures from web pages and repositories outside `research/papers/` remain unverified.

Metric conventions used throughout. The local backtest's "Brier of 0.829 (uniform 0.938)" is the multi-category Brier score, i.e. the sum over the 16 playoff teams of (p_team - outcome_team)^2, range 0 to 2; a uniform 1/16 forecast scores 15·(1/16)^2 + (15/16)^2 = 0.9375, which matches the stated 0.938 **[own computation]**. The uniform log loss on the champion is ln 16 = 2.773 **[own computation]**. All FiveThirtyEight numbers below use the same two definitions so they are directly comparable.

## Key question 1: How did FiveThirtyEight's NBA title and series forecasts score (own tracker, independent checks), and what pre-playoff title probability did they give eventual champions?

### Takeaway
FiveThirtyEight never published a title-level Brier for the NBA, but its raw forecast archive allows one to be computed: over the seven seasons 2015-16 to 2021-22, its pre-playoff title probabilities score a multi-category Brier of 0.912 (SE 0.129), a log loss of 2.11 (SE 0.33), the favourite won 1 of 7 titles, and the eventual champion was given a mean 0.177 probability **[own computation]**. That is worse than the local Elo backtest's 0.829 / 2.13 / 6 of 21 / 0.222, but on different and fewer seasons, and the standard errors are large enough that nothing can be concluded about which forecaster is better.

### Cited Findings

- The interactive version reports Brier skill scores versus "an unskilled estimate, such as a baseline based on historical averages", for forecasts back to 2008, says all its forecasts beat an unskilled guess, and that MLB had the lowest skill score; the search snippets did not expose the NBA skill score value — [FiveThirtyEight, "Checking our work"](https://projects.fivethirtyeight.com/checking-our-work)

### Inferences
- On the two headline numbers the local Elo backtest (0.829, 2.13, 21 seasons) and FiveThirtyEight (0.912, 2.11, 7 seasons) are statistically indistinguishable: FiveThirtyEight's 7-season Brier SE alone is 0.129, and the seasons differ. A fair comparison must score both on the same seasons (2015-16 to 2021-22 overlap the backtest window) and report the paired difference.
- FiveThirtyEight's pre-playoff favourites were much sharper than the Elo model's (mean favourite probability 0.419 vs the Elo model's reported mean champion probability of 0.222 and a favourite that wins 6 of 21), and the 2016-2022 period punished sharpness (favourite won 1 of 7; 2018 and 2019 Warriors, 2021 Bucks and 2022 Warriors were large misses). Over-confidence in the 0.2-0.5 range in the pooled-round calibration is consistent with FiveThirtyEight's own "overconfident in favorites" statement.
- The Elo model's score of 0.829 is below (better than) FiveThirtyEight's 0.912 largely because the uniform-like flatness of Elo forecasts is rewarded in a period of upsets; this is a calibration effect, not evidence of better discrimination.

### Gaps
- FiveThirtyEight's NBA Brier skill score in its interactive tracker was not visible in search snippets; the page itself could not be fetched.
- No published FiveThirtyEight series-level (best-of-seven) scoring was found; the archive would allow it (daily make_conf_semis etc. probabilities), but series start dates would need to be reconstructed from the game file.
- No pre-playoff FiveThirtyEight title probabilities exist for 2003-2015 or 2022-23 (forecasts began 2015-16 and the site's sports desk closed in 2023), so a 21-season head-to-head with the Elo backtest is impossible with FiveThirtyEight; a maximum of 7 overlapping seasons is available.

## Key question 2: How often has the pre-playoff betting favourite won the NBA title (2003-2025), at what implied probability, and how would a futures market score on Brier/log loss?

### Takeaway
Sources agree that the pre-playoff betting favourite has won roughly 70 percent of titles since 1980 (Covers: 25 titles since 1976), but only 3 of 7 from 2019 to 2025; no season-by-season table with odds could be extracted, and no one appears to have published a Brier or log-loss score for NBA title futures. A market forecast therefore scores far better than the Elo model on "favourite wins" but its full-distribution score is unknown and would have to be computed from archived odds.

### Cited Findings
- "Dating back to 1980, the pre-playoff betting favorite wins the championship roughly 70 percent of the time", but "from 2019 through 2025, pre-playoff favorites won just three of seven titles"; the 2019 Warriors at -220 "carrying the highest implied probability of any modern contender" lost the Finals; the 1996 and 1997 Bulls at -600 and -500 both won — [RealGM Wiretap (April 2026)](https://basketball.realgm.com/wiretap/285089/Pre-Playoff-Betting-Favorite-Has-Won-70-Percent-Of-NBA-Championships-Since-1980)
- Since 1976 the favourite entering the playoffs "has won the title 25 times; lost in the Finals 9 times", the rest losing earlier; in 2026 the favourite Oklahoma City (+115) lost in the conference finals and New York won at +2200 — [Covers / SportsOddsHistory](https://www.covers.com/sportsoddshistory/?p=47114). SportsOddsHistory also hosts "NBA Championship favorites entering the playoffs" back to 1969 and per-season futures tables — [Covers / SportsOddsHistory, Futures Odds of NBA Champions](https://www.covers.com/sportsoddshistory/nba-champs/)
- Preseason favourites: won 11 of 19 titles 1992-2010 and 5 of 10 from 2011 to 2020, "but that drops to four when measured by the favorite entering the postseason" because the 2015-16 Warriors became favourites after the season started and lost — [CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/nba-championship-odds-best-bets-to-walk-away-with-2021-title-among-leagues-top-teams); in 36 seasons to 2020 the preseason favourite won 18 times, and since 1985 no team outside the top eight in preseason odds has won — [Sportico](https://www.sportico.com/leagues/basketball/2020/lakers-title-odds-1234618883/); RealGM separately puts the preseason favourite at 50 percent since 1985 — [RealGM Wiretap](https://basketball.realgm.com/wiretap/261007/NBA-Preseason-Favorite-Has-Won-Finals-50-Percent-Of-Time-Since-1985)
- Season-specific pre-playoff market points found: 2004 Lakers favoured, Pistons won at +500 (one of the biggest Finals upsets) — [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/45560886/game-7-line-drops-pacers-try-nba-finals-upset-vs-thunder); 2018-19 Warriors -168 to -220 (two snapshots) — [Yahoo Sports](https://sports.yahoo.com/nba/article/2025-26-nba-finals-odds-oklahoma-city-thunder-open-as-biggest-title-favorites-since-kevin-durant-steph-curry-warriors-031505420.html), [RealGM](https://basketball.realgm.com/wiretap/285089/Pre-Playoff-Betting-Favorite-Has-Won-70-Percent-Of-NBA-Championships-Since-1980); 2023 entering playoffs: Bucks +250, Celtics +350, Suns +500, Warriors +850, 76ers +900, Nuggets +900 (about 9.1-9.5 percent implied before vig), Lakers +1200 — [SI Betting](https://www.si.com/betting/2023/04/11/nba-championship-futures-odds-milwaukee-bucks-boston-celtics), [Action Network](https://www.actionnetwork.com/nba/2023-nba-title-odds-betting-favorite-playoffs-bucks-celtics-suns-76ers-warriors); 2024 before the play-in: Celtics +160, 31.78 percent implied, and Boston won — [SI Betting](https://www.si.com/betting/every-team-s-nba-championship-odds-ahead-of-play-in-tournament-thunder-celtics-favored-01jrt9njthfn); 2026: Oklahoma City +115 lost, Knicks +2200 won — [Covers](https://www.covers.com/sportsoddshistory/?p=47114)
- Longest preseason odds of recent finalists (SportsOddsHistory): 2025-26 San Antonio +6600, 2024-25 Indiana +6600, 2021-22 Boston +4000, 2020-21 Phoenix +4000, 2019-20 Miami +6000 — [Sports Odds History on X](https://x.com/SOHistory/status/2060917669612904771)
- A TrueHoop piece argues settled betting lines "represent the sum of all models", the usual justification for using the market as the benchmark — [TrueHoop](https://www.truehoop.com/p/what-betting-markets-predict-in-the)
- No study scoring NBA title futures with Brier or log loss was found; the one methodological note located (a betting site) says Brier scores depend on the base rate and should be compared against a climatology baseline, and that futures odds must be converted (p = 100/(100+line) for positive lines) and normalised to remove the overround — search returned no primary source for an NBA futures backtest.
- Favourite-longshot bias literature: the standard overview is Ottaviani and Sørensen — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2008/the-favorite-longshot-bias-an-overview-of-the-main/?p=1); NBA-specific efficiency work covers totals (Baryla et al. 2007, early-season bias) and live betting, not title futures — [EMU](https://commons.emich.edu/accfin_facsch/90); NCAA tournament markets found efficient with little seeding bias (Hickman 2020) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jecfin/v44y2020i3d10.1007_s12197-020-09507-7.html)

### Inferences
- If the favourite wins about 70 percent of the time since 1980 at implied probabilities that (from the snapshots above) typically sit around 30-70 percent, the market is likely under-confident on favourites rather than over-confident; a market forecast with a 0.4-0.6 favourite that wins 60-70 percent of the time would score a multi-category Brier well below 0.8 on those seasons **[inference, not computed]**. The 2019-2025 stretch (3 of 7) would pull this toward the Elo model's numbers.
- The Elo model's favourite winning 6 of 21 (28.6 percent; Wilson 95 percent interval 13.8-50.0 percent **[own computation]**) is far below the market favourite's historical rate even allowing for the recent slump, which suggests the Elo model's discrimination (ordering) is weaker than the market's, not just its calibration.
- Vig removal matters: title futures carry a large overround across 16-30 teams, and a Brier computed on raw implied probabilities (summing well over 1) is not comparable to a model whose probabilities sum to 1.

### Gaps
- A season-by-season table of pre-playoff favourites with odds for 2003-04 to 2024-25 exists at SportsOddsHistory and inside the RealGM article but could not be opened; it needs to be pulled directly before any market Brier can be computed.
- No published Brier/log loss for NBA title futures over any period was found.
- Whether the 2026 season should be in scope (Knicks at +2200) depends on the report's window; the backtest ends 2023-24.

## Key question 3: What is the statistical power of comparing two championship forecasters over about 21 seasons, and what do forecasting researchers recommend?

### Takeaway
With one champion per season, 21 seasons cannot distinguish forecasters that differ by realistic amounts: my simulation shows that even a 50 percent over- or under-sharpening of a calibrated forecast changes the expected multi-category Brier by only 0.005-0.019 and the champion log loss by 0.03-0.09, against per-season standard deviations of 0.07-0.13 (Brier) and 0.24-0.48 (log loss), giving paired-test power of about 5-13 percent at N = 21 **[own computation]**. The literature recommends paired, event-level comparisons (Diebold-Mariano with small-sample corrections, permutation or bootstrap), scoring every series or every round rather than only the champion, and Brier skill scores against a stated baseline.

### Cited Findings
- Paired comparison: when two forecasts score the same events, the outcome-only term of the Brier difference cancels, so the comparison reduces to a per-event score difference — [arXiv physics/0401046, "The problem with the Brier score"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0401046)
- The Diebold-Mariano test is the standard paired t-type test on observation-level score differences — [statsmodels documentation](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.tsa.stattools.diebold_mariano_test.html); it "can be subject to large size distortions in small samples" because the long-run variance estimate is unreliable, motivating fixed-b/fixed-m asymptotics — [Coroneo and Iacone, York discussion paper](https://www.york.ac.uk/media/economics/documents/discussionpapers/2015/1515.pdf); the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold correction uses a t distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom — [statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.tsa.stattools.diebold_mariano_test.html); Döhrn (2019) finds permutation tests outperform Diebold-Mariano in small-sample simulations — [RWI paper via ULB Münster](https://epflicht.ulb.uni-muenster.de/download/pdf/643477)
- Sampling uncertainty of the Brier score has a closed form (Bradley, Schwartz and Hashino 2008, Weather and Forecasting); "with infrequently occurring events, a few hundred forecast-observation pairs may be needed to establish that a forecast is skillful" — [University of Iowa repository](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Sampling-Uncertainty-and-Confidence-Intervals-for/9983991990102771); Ferro's R code and 2007 paper give standard errors and bootstrap confidence intervals for comparing forecast systems by Brier score — [empslocal.ex.ac.uk brier.r](https://empslocal.ex.ac.uk/people/staff/ferro/Publications/brier.r); Siegert derives sampling variances for the reliability/resolution/uncertainty decomposition — [arXiv 1303.6182](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.6182); the empirical decomposition "provides a too optimistic estimate of the potential score" — [Reading eprints](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/29162)
- Practitioner guidance: a forecasting competition of "50-ish questions ... easily gives well over a 10 percent sampling error in the estimation of the Brier score", and a forecaster averaging 0.21 could score 0.16 over 30 questions by chance — [Entropic Thoughts, "Brier score"](https://entropicthoughts.com/brier-score)
- Recent forecasting benchmarks compare forecasters with paired, question-level bootstrap confidence intervals on the Brier difference — [arXiv 2608.28482](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28482); the AIA Forecaster report shows pairwise p-values of 0.15 and 0.09 for differences that look large in raw Brier terms — [arXiv 2511.07678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07678); the seqcomp R package offers confidence sequences/e-processes for comparing two forecasters as data arrive — [seqcomp vignette](https://cran.r-universe.dev/seqcomp/doc/seqcomp.html)
- Comparing Brier scores across events with different numbers of outcomes is unfair — [INSEAD working paper, "Fair skill Brier score"](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/working-papers/fair-skill-brier-score-evaluating-probabilistic); a paper on competitive-outcome prediction prefers log loss because it reads as a likelihood and is invariant to class relabelling, while "the Brier score is hard to interpret and may behave in unintuitive ways" — [arXiv 1701.08055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.08055)
- FiveThirtyEight's own practice for tournaments is to report Brier skill versus a chalk baseline and to note that "all the models beat the chalk baseline" — [FiveThirtyEight, NCAA forecast review](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-fivethirtyeights-ncaa-tournament-forecasts-did)
- Murphy decomposition Brier = reliability - resolution + uncertainty; reliability measures calibration, resolution rewards forecasts that vary with outcomes — [Wikipedia, Brier score](https://www.wikipedia.com/wiki/Brier_score)
- **[own computation]** The backtest's ± values are standard errors: `src/domain/backtesting/metrics.rs` divides the sample standard deviation across the 21 seasons by √21, so the per-season standard deviation behind 0.089 is 0.41 for Brier and behind 0.26 is 1.21 for log loss (both recomputed from the stored forecasts). The gaps between the Elo model (0.829), the better-seed model (0.828) and log5 (0.811) are 0.001-0.018, well inside one standard error. The per-season SD of 0.41 is larger than the 0.24 of the simulated calibrated forecaster below because the Elo forecasts are sharper than that forecaster; a paired test would use the SD of per-season differences, which is smaller (see the simulation).
- **[own computation, simulation of 200,000 seasons]** For a calibrated 16-team title forecast with favourite 0.35 and geometric tail, expected multi-category Brier is 0.787 (per-season SD 0.238), expected champion log loss 1.84 (SD 0.97), and the favourite wins 35 percent of the time. Scoring a second forecaster that is a tempered version of the truth (probabilities raised to a power then renormalised) against the calibrated one on the same seasons: power 0.75 (flatter) gives mean Brier difference +0.0072 (SD of paired difference 0.070) and log-loss difference +0.035 (SD 0.242); power 1.5 (sharper) gives +0.019 (SD 0.130) and +0.089 (SD 0.483). Two-sided 5 percent paired-test power at N = 21: 7 percent and 10 percent (power 0.75), 10 percent and 13 percent (power 1.5); at N = 100 seasons still only 17-31 percent (Brier) and 30-46 percent (log loss). Exponents of 0.9 or 1.1 give power at the nominal 4-5 percent level even at N = 100.

### Inferences
- A 21-season title-only comparison has essentially no power to rank the Elo model against the better-seed or log5 baselines, or against FiveThirtyEight or the market; the honest statement is that all of them are within noise of each other and of a calibrated forecast's expected score (about 0.79 for a 0.35 favourite, closer to 0.85-0.90 for flatter favourites **[inference from simulation]**).
- Power rises only by adding events: scoring all 15 series per season (315 series over 21 seasons) or all 64 team-round items per season (1,344 items), and by paired bootstrap or permutation tests on per-item differences. FiveThirtyEight's pooled-round binary Brier SE of 0.0086 over 7 seasons (above) illustrates the gain relative to a title-only SE of 0.129.
- Calibration and discrimination should be reported separately (reliability diagram plus resolution, or Brier skill versus both a uniform and a chalk/seed baseline); a flat forecast can win on Brier in an upset-heavy era while having less resolution, which is precisely the Elo-vs-FiveThirtyEight pattern in Key question 1.

### Gaps
- No published power analysis specific to sports championship forecasts was found; the power numbers above are my simulation under one assumed probability profile and should be re-run with the Elo model's actual per-season distributions.
- Whether the Diebold-Mariano test's distributional assumptions hold for 21 independent seasons was not addressed by any source found; a permutation test on per-season (or per-series) paired differences avoids the issue.

## Key question 4: What is the evidence on "better seed" and "regular-season record" baselines for predicting series winners, and how much better do rating-based models do?

### Takeaway
Higher seeds and home-court teams win about 74-77 percent of NBA series in recent decades (1-seeds beat 8-seeds about 93 percent of the time since 1984), so "better seed wins 75 percent" is a well-grounded baseline; the only direct test found of a ratings model against chalk (FiveThirtyEight's NCAA review) shows models beating chalk, and in the NBA FiveThirtyEight's game forecasts beat a coin flip by only about 0.03-0.04 Brier, so improvements over seed baselines at the series level are expected to be small.

### Cited Findings
- From 2009 to 2018 the team with home court won about 74 percent of playoff series; the author argues the seed gap rather than game location is the likely driver because first-round matchups are lopsided — [Samford University Center for Sports Analytics (2019)](https://www.samford.edu/sports-analytics/fans/2019/Quantifying-the-Home-Court-Advantage-in-the-NBA-Playoffs)
- Higher seeds have won 77.4 percent of conference quarter-final (first-round) series overall, 92.5 percent after taking a 2-0 lead; home teams won about 58 percent of playoff games over the last six postseasons (excluding the 2020 bubble), down from about 69 percent in the 15 seasons before — [AP via WSLS (April 2026)](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2026/04/22/road-teams-pushing-back-in-early-days-of-nba-playoffs-stealing-away-the-home-court-advantages/)
- In best-of-seven series, the team winning Game 1 at home has won the series 228-37 (about 86 percent); winning Game 1 on the road, 47-46 (about 50 percent); up 2-0 at home about 94 percent, on the road about 85 percent — [NBA.com (April 2018)](https://www.nba.com/news/nba-playoffs-notable-numbers-first-round-series-playoff-history)
- Since the 16-team format began in 1984, the No. 8 seed was 4-52 against the No. 1 seed in the first round and the No. 7 seed 5-51 against the No. 2 seed (article undated; written before later upsets) — [Philadelphia Inquirer, "Sixers Seeding Doesn't Matter"](https://inquirer.com/philly/blogs/boopstats/Sixers-Seeding-Doesnt-Matter0427.html); entering 2023 only five No. 8 seeds had ever won a series, and the same was true of No. 7 seeds over No. 2 seeds — [CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/2023-nba-playoffs-one-stat-proves-postseason-basketball-has-reached-historic-level-of-parity)

### Inferences
- A "better seed wins 75 percent" rule is close to the empirical first-round rate (74-77 percent) but too high for later rounds where seeds are closer; a seed-gap-dependent baseline (1-8 about 93 percent, 4-5 near 50 percent) is the stronger chalk baseline and would be harder for Elo to beat.
- Because Elo-type game forecasts beat a coin flip by only 0.03-0.04 Brier and trail the market by about 0.01, series-level and title-level gains over seed baselines should be expected to be a few hundredths at most, well inside the sampling noise of Key question 3.

## Key question 5: Is there published evidence that the NBA playoffs are more predictable than the regular season, and what does that imply for the metrics above?

### Takeaway
FiveThirtyEight's Elo work found that the better team wins more often in the playoffs than its regular-season edge implies (a 66 percent home favourite wins about 69 percent; series odds rise from 57 to 63 percent) and the NBA is the most "chalky" major league at the top seed, but on FiveThirtyEight's own 2016-2022 forecasts the playoff favourite won only slightly more often than in the regular season (67.1 vs 66.0 percent) with a worse Brier, so the extra predictability is modest and recent parity may have reduced it.

### Cited Findings
- FiveThirtyEight's Elo analysis: a team projected to win a regular-season home game 66 percent of the time wins about 69 percent of the time in the playoffs, and its seven-game series odds rise from 57 to 63 percent — [FiveThirtyEight, "Every cliche about the NBA playoffs is true"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/good-news-warriors-fans-every-cliche-about-the-nba-playoffs-is-true)
- FiveThirtyEight found a 0.776 correlation between preseason and playoff championship odds in the NBA, "far more stratified than other leagues" — [FiveThirtyEight, "Vegas thinks the NBA title race is as wide open as it's been in a long time"](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/vegas-thinks-the-nba-title-race-is-as-wide-open-as-its-been-in-a-long-time)
- The NBA has the lowest first-round exit rate for best-record teams among the four leagues (7.7 percent since 1995) — [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2023/04/20/sports/how-do-top-playoff-seeds-fare-postseason-across-four-mens-pro-leagues-it-can-vary)
- Counter-trend: CBS argues 2023 showed "historic" playoff parity and that great teams "can now emerge from just about anywhere on the bracket" — [CBS Sports](https://www.cbssports.com/nba/news/2023-nba-playoffs-one-stat-proves-postseason-basketball-has-reached-historic-level-of-parity); the pre-playoff favourite won only 3 of 7 titles 2019-2025 — [RealGM](https://basketball.realgm.com/wiretap/285089/Pre-Playoff-Betting-Favorite-Has-Won-70-Percent-Of-NBA-Championships-Since-1980); home teams' playoff game win rate fell to about 58 percent over the last six postseasons — [AP via WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2026/04/22/road-teams-pushing-back-in-early-days-of-nba-playoffs-stealing-away-the-home-court-advantages/)
- **[own computation from FiveThirtyEight data, 2016-2022]** Regular season: 8,302 games, favourite won 66.0 percent, mean favourite probability 0.697, Brier 0.2142 (over-confident). Playoffs: 584 games, favourite won 67.1 percent, mean favourite probability 0.661, Brier 0.2210 (slightly under-confident). By season the playoff favourite win rate ranged from 59.0 percent (2020 bubble, 83 games) to 74.4 percent (2016, 86 games) — [fivethirtyeight/checking-our-work-data, nba_games.csv](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/checking-our-work-data)
- Series format: analysts note the best-of-seven format favours the better team and best-of-five left more room for upsets — [The Analyst](https://theanalyst.com/articles/the-worst-teams-to-make-the-nba-finals-by-regular-season-record/), [The Ringer](https://www.theringer.com/2020/09/08/nba/biggest-playoff-upsets-nba-history)

### Inferences
- If playoff games are a few points more predictable than regular-season games, an Elo model tuned on regular-season games will be under-confident in the playoffs unless it applies a playoff multiplier (FiveThirtyEight's approach); the local tuning result (scale 0.75, home bonus 150 reducing over-confidence) points the other way and may reflect the 2019-2024 parity period rather than a structural property.
- Higher predictability at the game level compounds through best-of-seven series and four rounds, so title-level favourites should be sharper than game-level ones; a model whose favourite wins only 6 of 21 titles while a market favourite wins about 70 percent historically is leaving discrimination on the table.
- Because chalk is strong in the NBA, the right baseline for a title forecast is a seed- or record-based chalk model (like the backtest's 0.828 and 0.811), not the uniform 0.938; the Elo model's margin over chalk is within noise.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study comparing favourite win rates in NBA playoffs versus regular season over several decades was found; the FiveThirtyEight article is the only quantitative source, and the exact seasons and sample it used were not visible in the snippet.
- The 2023-24 and 2024-25 game-level data are absent from the FiveThirtyEight archive, so the 2016-2022 computation cannot be extended to the backtest's final seasons.
