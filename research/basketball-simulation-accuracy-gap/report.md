# Elo trails the market by 0.007 Brier

A FiveThirtyEight-style team-Elo Monte Carlo already sits within about **0.007 to 0.009 Brier and 2 percentage points of accuracy** of the best known NBA forecaster, the closing betting line, and nothing in the published literature closes that last gap with public data. This application's local simulator scores **66.0 percent favourite accuracy and a per-game Brier of 0.2128 over 26,457 regular-season games since 2003-04** (our_model_numbers.md); the no-vig closing moneyline scored 67.8 percent and Brier 0.2055 over 13,486 games in 2012-13 to 2022-23 ([DuyThucCao](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets)), and that market figure is the practical predictability ceiling because an encompassing regression gives the market a coefficient of 0.97 and an Elo model a coefficient of about zero on the same games. The MIT Sloan Sports Analytics Conference papers contain this benchmark exactly once: across 2007-2026 the Sloan basketball track produced a match simulator (Oh, Keshri and Iyengar 2015), a play-level season simulator that never forecasts game winners (Sandholtz and Bornn 2018) and a lineage of player-rating papers validated by out-of-sample game prediction (Sill 2010 onward), and one of them, the 2026 deep-reinforcement-learning valuation paper, publishes a game-winner accuracy against the Vegas closing line (66.3 percent for its player ratings and 67.1 percent with lineup synergies against 68.7 percent for the closing spread on 2,235 held-out games from 2020-21 to 2023-24, its Table 8); no Sloan paper publishes a log loss or Brier score against a betting line. Player-level, possession-level and machine-learning models cluster in the same 66-74 percent accuracy band as Elo, and both comparisons of a player-level model against closing odds had the model behind the bookmaker (0.68 versus 0.70 for Hubáček's network against Pinnacle; 67.1 versus 68.7 percent for the 2026 Sloan deep-reinforcement-learning ratings against the Vegas line). At the championship level this application's **21-season Brier of 0.829** is statistically indistinguishable from its own seed (0.828) and log5 (0.811) baselines and from FiveThirtyEight's pre-playoff title forecasts (0.912 over seven seasons), because one champion per season gives a paired test only 5-13 percent power. What the evidence supports fixing is the model's home-court term (100 Elo implies a 64 percent home win rate against an observed 58.3 percent), roster and injury awareness (worth 3.5-5.5 points of spread for a star), and treating team ratings as estimates with error rather than as known values in the playoff computation (the one study that measured this, Demsyn-Jones 2019, did so for pre-season playoff-qualification forecasts, where point-estimate ratings put 244 of 651 team-seasons below 1 or above 99 percent and drawing each rating error once per simulated season cut the mean squared error from 0.1323 to 0.1288; its size for series and title probabilities is unmeasured); what it does not support is more parameter tuning, more seasons of history, or richer possession simulation as a path past the market. One caveat governs the external numbers below: they were first read from search snippets and second-hand write-ups because the research session could not fetch full pages, and on 2026-10-23 the figures drawn from the 26 papers listed in `research/papers/README.md` were checked against the full text (see "Verified against the full papers"). That check changed four things of substance: a Sloan paper (Jenkins 2026) does score its game forecasts against the Vegas closing line and trails it by 1.6 points; the "better team wins about 80 percent of best-of-seven series" figure is not in Lopez, Matthews and Baumer and has been dropped; the Monte Carlo overconfidence study (Demsyn-Jones 2019) concerns pre-season playoff-qualification forecasts over 22 seasons, not series or title probabilities, so the "correlated rating error" recommendation has become a caution that point-estimate ratings understate uncertainty; and Gong and Chen's claimed prediction-accuracy gain does not exist in their paper. Figures taken from web pages and GitHub repositories that are not among those papers (the market comparisons, the FiveThirtyEight archive computations, the favourite counts) remain unverified against their sources.

## Sloan rewards new data and decisions, not forecast benchmarks

The Sloan conference is nearly the wrong place to look for an answer to "how accurate can an NBA game or title forecast be": across twenty years of the research track one paper, the 2026 deep-reinforcement-learning valuation, scores its game forecasts against a betting line, and that scarcity is itself the first finding. Across the 2007-2026 research track, the only paper whose stated purpose is simulating NBA matches is Oh, Keshri and Iyengar's 2015 **"Graphical Model for Basketball Match Simulation,"** which models each touch as a transition between discrete states over a network of the players on court, evaluates on 2013-14 data, and concludes that the approach "is appropriate for modeling a basketball match" and that "either changes in the team lineup or changes in the opponent team lineup significantly affects the dynamics of a match progression" (page 1) ([Sloan paper page](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/graphical-model-for-baskeball-match-simulation)); its only accuracy figures are at the team-season level, a within-sample R-squared of 0.92 and an out-of-sample R-squared of 0.87 between simulated and actual 2013-14 win percentages on a 70/30 split of the 1,230 regular-season games (page 5, Figure 3), with no game-level accuracy, log loss or market comparison, and a game is awarded to the team that wins the majority of repeated simulations rather than given a probability (page 5); Keshri's Columbia dissertation extends the evaluation to 2013-16 ([Columbia Academic Commons](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/d8-1ghx-zy51)). Sandholtz and Bornn's **"Replaying the NBA"** (Proceedings of the 12th SSAC, 2018; the version read is the Annals of Applied Statistics 2020 article "Markov Decision Processes with Dynamic Transition Probabilities: An Analysis of Shooting Strategies in Basketball," 14(3), 1122-1145) models every play that ends in a shot or turnover (fouls, free throws and rebounds are excluded) as a non-stationary Markov decision process with shot-clock-dependent transitions, assumes transitions are "invariant to the lineup" so that the chains can be combined into a team-average MDP (page 5), and simulates whole seasons at sub-second resolution, but its baseline is the on-policy simulation of the same team under altered shot policies, not a market or an Elo forecast ([arXiv 1812.05170](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.05170); [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.aoas/1600454859)).

The second Sloan lineage estimates team strength bottom-up from players and validates by predicting future games. Joseph Sill's 2010 **"Improved NBA Adjusted +/- Using Regularization and Out-of-Sample Testing,"** which won the best non-academic paper prize and is the origin of RAPM (the paper itself calls the method "regularization (a.k.a. ridge regression)" and never uses the term RAPM), reports a test RMSE of **11.47 points (R-squared 0.169)** on the margins of the March-April 2008-09 games for its best configuration (three seasons of data, lambda 3000, 1,200-minute cutoff; page 6), against 12.58 for predicting the mean home margin of 3.61 points and 12.01 (R-squared 0.088) for unregularized APM (page 5), and finds that "When using regularization, the benefit of additional years of data and the use of a minutes cutoff turns out to be modest" (page 6) ([paper PDF mirror](https://supermariogiacomazzo.github.io/STOR538_WEBSITE/Articles/Basketball/Basketball_Sill.pdf)). Gong and Chen's 2023 positional plus-minus paper estimates position-level means and variances of offensive and defensive plus-minus with a hierarchical Bayesian model over 2015-16 to 2021-22 (2019-20 excluded) and contains no prediction-accuracy claim and no out-of-sample test; the phrase "improved prediction accuracy in player plus-minus when factoring in player positions" attributed to its abstract does not appear in the paper ([Sloan paper page](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/estimating-positional-plus-minus-in-the-nba)), and the 2026 deep-reinforcement-learning valuation paper by Jenkins (the competition PDF is anonymised as "Paper ID 137") trains on a distributional win-probability model and reports, on 2,235 held-out games from 2020-21 to 2023-24, a game-winner accuracy of 66.3 percent (67.1 percent with pairwise synergies) against 64.8 percent for RAPM-based ratings, 62.4 percent for BPM and **68.7 percent for the Vegas closing line**, with margin RMSEs of 10.89 (10.71), 11.24, 11.87 and 10.42 (its Table 8, page 23); the paper attributes the remaining gap to "injury updates, rest management, travel fatigue, lineup changes, and market movements" (page 24) ([Sloan event page](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/event/deep-reinforcement-learning-for-nba-player-valuation-a-temporal-difference-approach-with-shapley-attribution)). The other game-winner accuracy comes from a Sloan-adjacent paper rather than a Sloan paper, Wolfson, Koopmeiners and DiLernia's **"Who's good this year?"** (arXiv 2015; Journal of Sports Analytics 2018, open access CC BY-NC 4.0; a 2012 Sloan proceeding is claimed by one citing source but not mentioned in the paper), which found the NBA the most predictable of the four major leagues, reached **over 65 percent accuracy using 87.5 percent of games for training**, observed home teams winning about 60 percent of NBA games, and found that information about team quality levels off once about 25 games per team are in the model (games sampled at random within the season, not in schedule order), and that margin of victory added virtually nothing for the NBA ([JSA 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/JSA-170199)). The only playoff-format simulation at Sloan is Rafique and Sanders' 2026 cross-sport tournament-design study (Oh, Keshri and Iyengar also ran a best-of-seven simulation of the 2014 Finals, but only as a two-lineup demonstration, page 7), which pairs closed-form probabilities with Monte Carlo under Bradley-Terry and Thurstone-Mosteller strengths and mentions the NBA only through the Play-In as an example of a hybrid Page-style design ([Sloan paper page](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/modern-playoff-tournament-design-analysis-across-sports-a-bayesian-modeling-and-advanced-simulation-study)).

Everything else on the basketball track is tracking-data description (rebounding in 2012, EPV in 2014, ball-screen defense in 2016, body pose in 2017, play sketching in 2018), draft and roster forecasting (2021, 2024, 2025), or strategy (foul trouble in 2011, three-point risk in 2013). FiveThirtyEight's own finalist count shows basketball finalists falling from four in 2013 and 2014 to two in 2015 and 2016 ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/the-big-ideas-from-this-years-sloan-conference)). The practical consequence for this application is that the forecast-accuracy benchmark literature lives in journals (International Journal of Forecasting 2012, Annals of Applied Statistics 2018, The American Statistician 2022, Journal of Sports Analytics) and on arXiv, and that Sloan's consistent message, that lineups matter (Oh, Keshri and Iyengar's two-lineup Finals demonstration; the 0.8 points of accuracy that pairwise synergies add in Jenkins 2026; Sandholtz and Bornn, by contrast, assume lineup invariance) and that regularization rather than more data makes ratings predictive, points at roster state as the omitted variable in any team-level Elo. The inventory is also incomplete: the research session could not open any per-year Sloan papers page, so 2007-2009, parts of 2013-2014, 2016-2017 and 2019-2020 were only partially visible.

## This application's 66 percent and 0.213 are exactly Elo-tier, 0.007 Brier behind the market

Every independent evaluation of a FiveThirtyEight-style NBA Elo found by the research lands in the same narrow band, and this application's model lands in it too. A from-scratch Elo on 7,641 games from 2010-2015 scored 67.2 percent against 67.3 percent for FiveThirtyEight's published forecast ([gakoduah](https://github.com/gakoduah/NBA-Game-Prediction-Elo)); Nic Dobson's replication "matched the original results" at 67-68 percent and found Elo "really robust to configuration" across K of 20, 35 and 50 and home court of 50 to 150 ([nicidob](https://nicidob.github.io/nba_elo/)); a walk-forward Elo over 13,486 out-of-sample games in 2012-13 to 2022-23 scored **Brier 0.2146 and log loss 0.6177** ([DuyThucCao](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets)); another Elo-plus-schedule model scored Brier 0.2126, log loss 0.6131 and 66.1 percent on unstated seasons ([nhorto/NBA](https://github.com/nhorto/NBA)); and FiveThirtyEight's own game forecasts in its public archive scored **Brier 0.2142 over 8,302 regular-season games in 2016-2022 with the favourite winning 66.0 percent** (own computation from [fivethirtyeight/checking-our-work-data](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/checking-our-work-data)). This application's **66.0 percent, Brier 0.2128 and log loss 0.6136 over 26,457 regular-season games from 2003-04 to 2024-25** (our_model_numbers.md) are therefore not a weak implementation; they are what this model class does.

Read against the market rows, this application's gap is **1.8 percentage points of accuracy, 0.0073 Brier and 0.017 log loss** (computed from the table; samples differ). In relative terms the model recovers about **84 percent of the market's improvement over a coin flip** on Brier (0.0372 of 0.0445, computed). The gap is small but systematic: the market beat the Elo model in every one of the eleven test seasons, and the encompassing regression's market coefficient of 0.97 (SE 0.045) against a model coefficient of -0.001 (SE 0.047, p 0.98) means the closing price already contains everything Elo knows ([DuyThucCao](https://github.com/DuyThucCao/nba-win-probability-markets)). Two further comparisons agree in direction and magnitude: TeamRankings pre-game probabilities scored Brier 0.2167 against FiveThirtyEight Elo's 0.2179 over the 2018-19 and 2019-20 regular seasons ([Maddox, Sides and Harvill, arXiv 2207.05114, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.05114)), and a Bradley-Terry model scored 0.213-0.219 against betting odds at 0.203 on 2018-19 games ([R-bloggers](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2022/02/comparing-the-bradley-terry-model-to-betting-odds/amp/)).

This application's calibration table explains the part of this gap that is under the model's control. In the 0.5 to 0.9 forecast bins, covering 18,684 of the 26,457 games, the mean forecast runs **3 to 5 percentage points above the observed home win rate** (0.552 forecast versus 0.503 observed in the 0.5-0.6 bin; 0.651 versus 0.602; 0.749 versus 0.706; 0.846 versus 0.812) (our_model_numbers.md). The fixed 100-Elo home bonus is the cause: 100 Elo implies a **64.0 percent** win probability for equal teams and 70 Elo implies 59.9 percent (computed), while the home team won 58.3 percent of regular-season games over the backtest window (our_model_numbers.md), which corresponds to roughly 58 Elo (computed). The published record agrees. Maymin, Maymin and Shen's win-probability model on 2006-07 to 2009-10 play-by-play gives the home team a 62 percent chance at tip-off against an evenly matched opponent (International Journal of Sport Finance 2012, page 12 of the PDF), FiveThirtyEight set home court at 100 Elo, about 3.5 points, and rejected a dynamic version "because it made almost no difference to the overall ratings" ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-we-calculate-nba-elo-ratings)), yet Dobson argued the bonus "should probably be +70" because the home team wins about 60 percent ([nicidob](https://nicidob.github.io/nba_elo/)), a 2019-20 residual analysis of 679 games found "the tendency for FiveThirtyEight to overweight home court advantage is clear" ([vandomed](https://vandomed.medium.com/fivethirtyeights-nba-predictions-raptor-vs-elo-254de5278645)), and a 2017-18 check found games given a 70-74 percent chance were won only about 61 percent of the time ([Jake Galant](https://medium.com/@jakegalant/how-did-fivethirtyeight-nba-predictions-do-in-the-2017-2018-season-f60fecf576ba)). The home win rate has fallen from 68.5 percent in 1976-77 and 62.8 percent in 2002-03 to 53.7 percent in 2014-15 ([ESPN](https://africa.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/12241619/home-court-advantage-decline)), about 54 percent in 2020-21 and near 54 percent in most seasons since except 58 percent in 2022-23 ([Boston 25 News](https://www.boston25news.com/news/national/fact-or-fiction-is/NVE32FOQOEVRYBXQSAJNA6OTJA/)). The papers read for this report give the same range: 59.27 percent over 2004-05 to 2019-20 (Migliorati, Table 3), about 59 percent over 7,376 games of 2012-13 to 2017-18 (Maddox, Sides and Harvill), 59.3 percent over 2008-2017 (Yeh, Rice and Dubin) and 60.73 percent of the last 410 games of 2010-11 (Omidiran, Table 2); Migliorati's best Elo fit used a home-court parameter of 40 Elo rather than 100, and Petridis and Pelechrinis cite the betting market's home edge at about 2 points against the 3.5 that 100 Elo implies. That decline is the most plausible driver of this application's per-season accuracy drift from 68-69 percent in 2008-2011 and 2016 to 61-65 percent in 2021-2025, with the 2020-21 season worst at 60.8 percent and Brier 0.2336 (our_model_numbers.md): a 1946-47 constant is being applied to a league where home court is worth half what it was. A season-dependent or decayed home-court term is the lowest-risk change the evidence supports, but its payoff is bounded; Dobson's finding that accuracy is flat across wide K and home-court ranges means parameter tuning moves Brier by thousandths, not by the 0.007-0.009 that separates Elo from the market.

## Perfect knowledge of team strength still calls only about 68 percent of games

Several independent lines of evidence converge on the conclusion that the market's 68 percent is not a soft target but an estimate of what an oracle on team strength could achieve. If the closing line is calibrated, which a study of Pinnacle moneylines over 2016-17 to 2019-20 supports by finding implied and actual win probabilities "don't differ by more than 5 percent" in any bin ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/why-you-are-betting-on-the-wrong-nba-teams-39e2bf98588/)), and if it contains all available information, which the encompassing coefficient of 0.97 supports, then its observed 67.8-68.1 percent accuracy is an unbiased estimate of the accuracy of a forecaster who knows the true pre-game probabilities, and about a third of regular-season games are coin-flip losses for everyone. Aoki, Assunção and Vaz de Melo reach the same figure from a non-market direction: across 198 leagues and 1,503 seasons they estimate the NBA underdog wins with probability **about 0.36**, stable across seasons, with the underdog winning 27 percent of games away and 45 percent at home (the abstract's 0.09 is the difference between 0.45 and the unconditional 0.36; the body gives the home-versus-away swing as 0.18), and conclude that "luck is substantially present even in the most competitive championships, which partially explains why sophisticated and complex feature-based models hardly beat simple models" ([arXiv 1706.02447](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02447)). Lopez, Matthews and Baumer, fitting Bayesian state-space models to a decade of betting-market probabilities, find the NBA has "both the largest dispersion in talent and the largest home advantage" of the four leagues, that the better of two randomly chosen teams has a median probability of about 67 percent of winning a neutral-site game (NFL about 64, NHL about 57, MLB about 56), and that a typical NBA home team beats an equal opponent 62.0 percent of the time, ranging from 60 percent for Brooklyn to 66.1 percent for Denver ([arXiv 1701.05976](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.05976)). The higher "glass ceiling" of **74-75 percent** that circulates in the machine-learning literature comes from Zimmermann and co-authors on college basketball, whose season-specific NCAA limits are 71-76 percent and whose paper names the NBA only as future work ([arXiv 1310.3607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1310.3607); [arXiv 1508.04688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.04688), Table 4 and p. 10), and the one NBA result at that level, Loeffelholz, Bednar and Bauer's 74.3 percent neural network on 650 games of 2007-08 (620 for training and testing, 30 held out), against 68.7 percent for USA Today's experts ([arXiv 1912.11762](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.11762)), was later called "surprising" by Zimmermann himself given its extensive tuning ([Zimmermann 2016](https://zimmermanna.users.greyc.fr/papers/journals/sadm2016.pdf)). For the NBA, 68-70 percent is the realistic ceiling; 74-75 percent is a college-basketball figure, and the one NBA result at that level was heavily tuned on a single season.

The richer model classes one might envy do not break through this ceiling, and the evidence is more consistent than the marketing around them. FiveThirtyEight moved from pure Elo to CARMELO and then RAPTOR precisely because Elo "only knows who won each game, the margin of victory and where the game was played" and cannot see injuries, trades or rest ([FiveThirtyEight methodology](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-our-nba-predictions-work/)), yet in the one season both ran publicly, 2019-20, Elo picked 340 of 503 winners (67.6 percent) against RAPTOR's 331 (65.8 percent) with no significant difference ([Andrew Kyne](https://andrewkyne.wordpress.com/2020/01/04/fivethirtyeight-nba-forecast-accuracy-raptor/)), and on 679 games RAPTOR scored 66.4 percent against Elo's 67.0 ([vandomed](https://vandomed.medium.com/fivethirtyeights-nba-predictions-raptor-vs-elo-254de5278645)). FiveThirtyEight's final blend kept Elo at roughly 35 percent weight against 65 percent RAPTOR talent, according to a mirrored copy of its text that could not be verified against the original ([text mirror of the FiveThirtyEight page, unverified](https://www.bit-a.jp/1bf108/fivethirtyeight-nba-prediction-accuracy)). Its CARM-Elo spreads differed from Vegas in 1,136 of 1,230 games in 2016-17 and betting the FiveThirtyEight side went 559-560-17, "no better than a coin flip" ([Stanford Sports Analytics](https://stanfordsportsanalytics.wordpress.com/2017/06/18/in-search-of-a-winning-strategy-comparing-fivethirtyeight-coms-carm-elo-predictions-to-las-vegas-point-spreads/)), and on playoff-berth probabilities it scored Brier 0.143 against the bookmakers' 0.139, p 0.881 ([R-bloggers](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2021/12/predicting-nba-playoff-berths-fivethirtyeight-vs-betting-markets/)). The player-level convolutional network of Hubáček, Šourek and Železný, published in the International Journal of Forecasting, profited only by deliberately decorrelating from bookmaker odds and sizing bets (claim from the 2019 paper's abstract, not verified against its text); when the same model's predictions for 16,000 games from 2000 to 2015 were scored against Pinnacle closing money lines (average margin 3.8 percent) it was **0.68 accurate against the bookmaker's 0.70**, "not as accurate as the bookmaker, and ... thus in a general KL-disadvantage", and only fractional-Kelly staking kept it profitable ([Uhrín, Šourek, Hubáček and Železný, arXiv 2107.08827, Table 3 p. 14 and Table 6 p. 17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.08827)). The earlier player-rating attempt on the line, Omidiran's 2013 penalized regression, won 57.24 percent of 290 bets against the Las Vegas spread in one season when it disagreed with the line by more than 3 points, which is not significant against the 52.5 percent break-even (z about 1.6, own computation), and its margin error was a mean absolute error of 10.55 points against 10.54 for a dummy that rates every player zero and gives the home team 3.5 points ([Omidiran, arXiv 1301.3523, Tables 2 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3523)). Possession-level simulation fares no better: Štrumbelj and Vračar's homogeneous Markov possession model, benchmarked against logit regression, a latent-strength rating and bookmaker odds, was "comparable in quality" to the statistical methods while "bookmaker odds were the best probabilistic forecasts" ([IAOR](https://iaorifors.com/paper/76013)), and a 2023 student possession-based scoring model reached about 60 percent against about 65 percent for existing models ([JHU project](https://designday.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/formidable/18/Predictive-Model-and-Algorithm-for-NBA-Games-2023-1-1.pdf)). Lineup models face an arithmetic wall: a team uses more than 600 lineups per season, so the average lineup has only a few dozen possessions of data (the paper's abstract says 25-30, its body about 17 on offense and 17 on defense with a standard deviation of 56, its conclusion 30-40) ([Petridis and Pelechrinis, arXiv 2601.15000, pp. 1 and 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15000)). Migliorati's cross-validated deep-learning study over 16 seasons even found that a single ex-ante Elo feature (accuracy 67.4 percent, AUC 0.712, best fit with a home-court parameter of 40 Elo rather than 100) beat eight Four Factors box-score predictors (64.0 percent, AUC 0.666) ([arXiv 2111.09695](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09695v1)), and a kernel-smoothed dynamic Bradley-Terry rating fitted to wins and losses only edged FiveThirtyEight's Elo by 0.27 points of pick accuracy (63.82 against 63.55 percent over 2016-17 to 2018-19) at one bandwidth and trailed it at the others ([Tian, Shi, Shen and Song, arXiv 2307.16642, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.16642)). Claims well above 75 percent, such as a 2025 PLOS One multilayer perceptron at 98.90 percent ([PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=printable&id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0326326)) or an LSTM at 72.35 percent that cites a 55 percent betting-line baseline ([arXiv 2512.08591](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.08591)), almost always use same-game features, in-sample fits or a mis-stated market baseline; a 2025 study found leakage inflated performance in nearly half the datasets tested ([BRACIS](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/bracis/article/view/40816)).

Where richer models do add value is concentrated, not spread across all games. Oddsmakers priced Nikola Jokic at **5.5 points** of spread, Shai Gilgeous-Alexander and Victor Wembanyama at 4 and Luka Doncic at 3.5 in the 2025 playoffs, with San Antonio moving from 1.5-point to 5.5-point favourites in one game when Wembanyama returned ([theScore](https://www.thescore.com/nba/news/3531319/nba-playoff-injury-woes-how-much-are-stars-worth-to-their-teams)). Against a home-court term worth about 3.5 points, a star absence mis-sets a team-Elo forecast by more than its entire home advantage, and a Finance Research Letters study of 10,555 games over nine seasons found 5,628 with at least one absence, "substantial biases when a (high-quality) player is absent" in opening lines, and "all biases are removed by game time" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1544612315000227)). Availability data therefore closes most of the Elo-to-market gap on the roughly half of games with an absence, but because closing lines already price absences fully it cannot carry a model past the market. Rest is a second-order effect: back-to-back games show a win rate of about 44 percent ([arXiv 2606.27957](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27957)), and FiveThirtyEight's CARM-Elo travel penalty of about 16 points for a Boston-to-Los Angeles leg is about one-sixth of home court ([FiveThirtyEight 2015-16](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-our-2015-16-nba-predictions-work)). Bowman, Harmon and Ashman's logistic model over 2000-01 to 2018-19 puts the size on a published footing: a visitor on a back-to-back raises the home team's win chance by 7.7 points on average and a home team on a back-to-back lowers it by 4.1, yet the schedule's net effect on any team's championship probability is under 0.01 per season (Journal of Sports Analytics 2023, Tables 1, 2 and 5), so rest belongs in a per-game forecast and can be left out of a title forecast. No study retrieved reports accuracy, Brier or log loss with and without injury features for NBA games, so the gain remains unquantified in the literature.

## Twenty-one champions cannot separate the title models at all

This application's championship backtest scores the Elo model at a **multi-category Brier of 0.829 ± 0.089 over the 16 playoff teams and a champion log loss of 2.13 ± 0.26 across 21 seasons from 2003-04 to 2023-24, with the favourite winning 6 of 21 and a mean probability on the eventual winner of 0.222**, against a uniform forecast at 0.938 and 2.77, a log5 record model at 0.811 and 1.83, a better-seed-wins-75-percent model at 0.828 and 1.91, and a rolling-tuned Elo at 0.815 and 2.08 (our_model_numbers.md). The arithmetic checks: a uniform 1/16 forecast scores exactly 0.9375 and ln 16 is 2.773 (own computation). This application's note already says the differences between non-uniform models are inside the standard errors, and the research confirms this is not a shortcoming of the backtest but a property of the problem. A simulation of 200,000 seasons for a calibrated 16-team forecast with a 0.35 favourite gives an expected Brier of 0.787 with a per-season standard deviation of 0.238 and an expected log loss of 1.84 with standard deviation 0.97; scoring a second forecaster that is a flattened (power 0.75) or sharpened (power 1.5) version of the truth on the same seasons gives mean Brier differences of only 0.007 and 0.019 against paired-difference standard deviations of 0.070 and 0.130, so a two-sided paired test at N 21 has **power of about 5-13 percent**, and even at N 100 only 17-46 percent (own simulation; `scripts/power.py`). The ± values are standard errors (the backtest divides the sample standard deviation across seasons by the square root of their number), so the implied per-season standard deviation is 0.41, larger than the 0.24 of the simulated calibrated forecaster below because the Elo forecasts are sharper than it; whereas if they were standard deviations, the 21-season standard error would be 0.019 for Brier and 0.057 for log loss. This application's file says "standard errors from the spread across seasons," and either way the 0.001-0.018 gaps between models are inside the noise.

The same noise swallows the comparison with FiveThirtyEight, which is the only external title forecaster whose full distribution was obtainable. From its public archive, taking for each season the first date on which exactly 16 teams were in the playoffs and no series was decided, FiveThirtyEight's pre-playoff title forecasts over 2015-16 to 2021-22 score a **Brier of 0.912 (SE 0.129) and log loss of 2.11 (SE 0.33); the favourite won 1 of 7 titles and the champion was given a mean 0.177 probability** while the favourite averaged 0.419 (own computation from [fivethirtyeight/checking-our-work-data](https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/checking-our-work-data)). The season-by-season misses are large: Warriors 0.425 and Cavaliers 0.079 in 2016; Rockets 0.438 and the champion Warriors 0.043 in 2018 with Curry injured; Warriors 0.601 and Raptors 0.163 in 2019; Clippers 0.268 and Bucks 0.066 in 2021; Celtics 0.321 and Warriors 0.086 in 2022. FiveThirtyEight itself said in 2020 that its NBA models "tend to be overconfident in favorites" ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-well-did-our-sports-predictions-hold-up-during-a-year-of-chaos/)), and the pooled four-round calibration over 448 team-round items shows the 0.2-0.5 bins, where title favourites sit, running over-confident (0.250 predicted against 0.171 observed on 41 items; 0.439 against 0.333 on 24), though bins that small prove nothing (own computation). This application's Elo therefore scores better than FiveThirtyEight (0.829 against 0.912) mainly because flat forecasts are rewarded in an upset-heavy era; this is a calibration effect, not evidence of better discrimination, and the seasons do not even overlap fully, so no ranking is justified.

The one dimension on which this application's model does look weak is discrimination, and the market is the yardstick. Since 1980 the pre-playoff betting favourite has won "roughly 70 percent" of titles, although only 3 of 7 from 2019 through 2025 ([RealGM](https://basketball.realgm.com/wiretap/285089/Pre-Playoff-Betting-Favorite-Has-Won-70-Percent-Of-NBA-Championships-Since-1980)), and since 1976 the favourite entering the playoffs has won 25 times ([Covers/SportsOddsHistory](https://www.covers.com/sportsoddshistory/?p=47114)). The Elo favourite's 6 of 21 (28.6 percent; Wilson 95 percent interval 13.8-50.0 percent, own computation) is far below that even allowing for the recent slump, and the Elo model's mean favourite probability is well below FiveThirtyEight's 0.419. This matches the structural evidence that the NBA is chalky: a conference No. 1 seed has won about two-thirds of titles ([Land of Basketball](https://www.landofbasketball.com/championships/champions_by_seed.htm)), the team with home court won 231 of 305 best-of-seven series (75.7 percent) from 1947 to 2005 (Rump 2006, Table 4, own sum), the better-record team won 73.4 percent of 68 Finals from 1951 to 2018 (Chance 2020, Table 2) and higher seeds won 72.9 percent of first-round series from 2010-11 to 2015-16 (Wolfson, Koopmeiners and DiLernia 2018), higher seeds won 77.4 percent of first-round series ([AP via WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2026/04/22/road-teams-pushing-back-in-early-days-of-nba-playoffs-stealing-away-the-home-court-advantages/)), the team with home court won about 74 percent of series from 2009 to 2018 ([Samford](https://www.samford.edu/sports-analytics/fans/2019/Quantifying-the-Home-Court-Advantage-in-the-NBA-Playoffs)), and a team FiveThirtyEight's Elo projected to win a regular-season home game 66 percent of the time won about 69 percent in the playoffs, lifting its series odds from 57 to 63 percent ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/good-news-warriors-fans-every-cliche-about-the-nba-playoffs-is-true)). This application's own data pulls the other way, however: its playoff Brier (0.2164) is worse than its regular-season Brier, and its rolling backtest selects a scale of 0.75, flatter probabilities, for the playoffs (our_model_numbers.md), and FiveThirtyEight's 2016-2022 playoff forecasts also scored worse than its regular-season ones (Brier 0.2210 over 584 games against 0.2142) with the playoff favourite winning only 67.1 percent against 66.0 (own computation from the archive). The most consistent reading is that the chalk advantage is real but has shrunk in the parity era, and that a 1946-vintage home term, which bites hardest in the playoffs where home teams won only about 58 percent of games over the last six postseasons against about 69 percent in the fifteen before ([AP via WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2026/04/22/road-teams-pushing-back-in-early-days-of-nba-playoffs-stealing-away-the-home-court-advantages/)), is what the 0.75 scale is compensating for. No published Brier or log loss for NBA title futures exists, and the season-by-season odds table at SportsOddsHistory could not be opened in the research session, so the market's full-distribution title score is the single most valuable number still to compute.

## What closes the gap, what does not, and how to tell

The evidence sorts candidate improvements into three bins. The first bin, changes that measurably reduce this application's error, is short. A season-dependent home-court term is justified by this application's own calibration table and by every independent critique of FiveThirtyEight's fixed 100 Elo; its payoff is a few thousandths of Brier concentrated in the 2015-2025 seasons and a flatter playoff forecast without an ad hoc 0.75 scale. Roster and injury awareness is the omitted variable that every source names, from FiveThirtyEight's own reason for abandoning pure Elo to Neil Paine's post-FiveThirtyEight model, which blends ratings simulations with FanDuel implied probabilities explicitly because "a statistical power rating-based system cannot readily account for" injuries and trades ([Paine 2024-25](https://neilpaine.substack.com/p/2024-25-nba-forecast)); the Sloan lineup papers and the market's 3.5-5.5-point star prices put an upper bound on its value of "most of the remaining 0.007 Brier, on the half of games with an absence." Rating uncertainty is the third, and the evidence for it is narrower than the first draft of this report implied: Demsyn-Jones (2019) built pre-season Monte Carlo models of 22 NBA regular seasons (1995-96 to 2016-17), each evaluated out of time, and found "systematic and substantial overconfidence in playoff probabilities, which can be eliminated by incorporating error correlation"; the probabilities are of qualifying for the playoffs, 244 of 651 team-seasons were given under 1 or over 99 percent, teams given under 10 percent qualified five times as often as predicted, and the fix is to draw each player's rating and minutes error once per simulated season and hold it fixed for all 82 games ([Demsyn-Jones 2019, JSA 5:1-15, CC BY-NC 4.0](https://doi.org/10.3233/JSA-170220)), and this application's exact-bracket computation, which takes its point-estimate ratings as known, has the same structure in principle, although the paper measures the effect only for pre-season playoff-qualification forecasts, not for series or title probabilities, and its fix flattens probabilities, which would lower a title forecast whose favourites already win only 6 of 21. Within-series effects the literature supports are a home/away split in the per-game probability (Rump 2006 fits 305 NBA series with the favourite at 0.736 at home and 0.445 away and rejects the single-probability binomial, while showing that with independent games the home-away order changes the series length, not the series winner) and series state, where the team winning Game 1 at home has won the series 228-37 ([NBA.com](https://www.nba.com/news/nba-playoffs-notable-numbers-first-round-series-playoff-history)); momentum appears in NBA Finals data as negative serial correlation, with the team that wins one game more likely to lose the next ([JORS 2012](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1057/jors.2011.92)); Chance (2020) finds the same in 68 Finals (the team up 1-0 wins Game 2 47 percent of the time, up 2-0 wins Game 3 38 percent) and attributes it to venue alternation rather than momentum, which argues against hot-hand adjustments.

The second bin, changes that will not close the gap, is longer and worth stating because each is tempting. Tuning K or the margin multiplier will not: Elo is "really robust to configuration" ([nicidob](https://nicidob.github.io/nba_elo/)) and FiveThirtyEight found a dynamic home term made "almost no difference" ([FiveThirtyEight](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-we-calculate-nba-elo-ratings)). More history will not: Sill found extra years modest once regularized, Wolfson, Koopmeiners and DiLernia found information levels off at about 25 games per team, and the market already has every public game result. Replacing Elo with a possession or play simulator will not, on the evidence of Štrumbelj and Vračar and the Johns Hopkins model, because the extra structure does not add information the team result did not already carry. Chasing published machine-learning accuracies above 72 percent will not, because the credible NBA ceiling is 68-70 percent and the higher claims fail leakage or baseline checks. Building a player-level model to beat the market will not either: the strongest operator of one kept Elo at a third of the weight, and the two closing-line comparisons lost 0.68 to 0.70 and 67.1 to 68.7 percent. The only thing that moves a public model to market-tier is the market itself, which is why Paine's default is a composite with FanDuel odds and why the Elo coefficient in the encompassing regression is zero.

The third bin is measurement, and it is where this application's project can make the largest honest gain. A title-only score over 21 seasons has 5-13 percent power, so the backtest should score every series (15 per season, 315 over 21 seasons) or every team-round item (64 per season, 1,344 in total); FiveThirtyEight's pooled four-round binary Brier of 0.0981 per item had a standard error of 0.0086 over seven seasons against a title-only standard error of 0.129 (own computation from the archive). Comparisons should be paired on the same events, where the outcome term of the Brier difference cancels ([arXiv physics/0401046](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0401046)), and tested by permutation or bootstrap rather than a plain t-test, since Döhrn finds permutation tests outperform Diebold-Mariano in small samples ([RWI](https://epflicht.ulb.uni-muenster.de/download/pdf/643477)). Calibration and discrimination should be reported separately through the Murphy decomposition, because a flat forecast can win on Brier in an upset era while having less resolution, which is precisely the Elo-versus-FiveThirtyEight pattern above. The chalk baseline should be seed-gap-dependent rather than a flat 75 percent, since No. 8 seeds went 4-52 against No. 1 seeds from 1984 ([Philadelphia Inquirer](https://inquirer.com/philly/blogs/boopstats/Sixers-Seeding-Doesnt-Matter0427.html)) while 4-5 series are near coin flips, and a stronger chalk is harder for Elo to beat. Finally, the market should be scored on the same seasons: a no-vig closing-line Brier on the 2003-04 to 2024-25 regular-season games and a vig-removed futures Brier on the 21 playoff fields would replace the cross-sample comparisons in this report with paired ones.

## Conclusion

The question "how far is a team-Elo Monte Carlo from the best basketball models" has a sharper answer than the literature's size suggests: about 0.007-0.009 Brier at the game level, inside the noise at the title level, and the thing on the other side of the gap is not a better model but the betting market, which absorbs the private information (injury reports, lineup news, sharp money) that no public rating can see. The Sloan corpus, read for this purpose, is a decade and a half of evidence that the basketball analytics community moved to lineups, tracking and decisions because the game-forecast problem was already saturated; the papers that do touch forecasting all land in the same mid-60s band this application's model occupies, and the one that measured itself against a closing line, the 2026 deep-reinforcement-learning paper at 67.1 against 68.7 percent, trailed it by the same 1.6-1.8 points that separate Elo from the market. That reframes this application's 66 percent and 0.213 from a shortfall into a confirmation that the implementation is faithful, and it reframes the championship backtest's indistinguishable 0.811-0.829 cluster as a correct result that the test design cannot resolve rather than a model failure.

The implication for the project is to spend effort on the three things the evidence rewards, a season-aware home-court term, a bracket computation that treats ratings as estimates with error rather than as known values (point-estimate ratings understate uncertainty; the measured effect, on pre-season playoff-qualification forecasts, was a mean-squared-error gain of 0.0035 concentrated in the under-10 and over-80 percent bins, and its size for series and title probabilities is unmeasured), and a roster-availability input or a market composite for live forecasts, while redirecting evaluation effort from the champion to the series and from raw Brier to paired, decomposed scores against a seed-gap chalk and a vig-removed market. The external numbers here were first read from search snippets because the research environment could not fetch full pages. On 2026-10-23 the figures drawn from the 26 papers listed in `research/papers/README.md` were checked against the full text, and the section above lists each verdict. In substance the check added a Sloan closing-line benchmark (Jenkins 2026, 67.1 against 68.7 percent on 2,235 games), removed the 80 percent series figure that Lopez, Matthews and Baumer never state, narrowed the Monte Carlo overconfidence finding to pre-season playoff qualification, withdrew the prediction-accuracy claim attributed to Gong and Chen, restated Rump's result as one about series length rather than the winner, placed the 74-75 percent ceiling in college basketball, and resolved the Oh-Keshri-Iyengar question (their only accuracy is a team-season win-percentage R-squared of 0.87). The figures that still rest on web pages and repositories outside that list are unchanged and unverified; the document most worth reading in full before it is cited as settled is now the SportsOddsHistory pre-playoff favourites table, for the market title score that nobody has yet published.
